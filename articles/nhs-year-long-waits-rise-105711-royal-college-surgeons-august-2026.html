NHS Year-Long Waits Jump By More Than 11,000 As Progress Stalls

After ten consecutive months of falling year-long waits, the direction has reversed. More than 105,000 cases in England have now been waiting over a year for planned treatment — and the rise was recorded in every single region.

A year is a long time to wait for an operation. It is long enough to lose a job, to give up a hobby, to watch a manageable condition harden into something that shapes the rest of your life. It is long enough for the pain to stop being an event and start being a personality.

More than one hundred thousand people in England are currently in that position — and for the first time in nearly a year, that number is going in the wrong direction.

What The Surgeons Found

Analysis published this month by the Royal College of Surgeons of England shows that the number of cases where a patient has been waiting more than 52 weeks for planned NHS treatment rose from 94,404 in March 2026 to 105,711 in June 2026. That is an increase of 11,307 cases, or 11.98%, in three months.

The context makes it worse rather than better. This rise follows ten consecutive months of falling year-long waits. Whatever was working has stopped working. The College is blunt about what that means: progress on the longest waits has stalled.

Nor is this a local difficulty that can be explained away by one struggling trust or one difficult patch of the country. The increase was recorded across every region of England. When a problem appears everywhere at once, it is not a regional failure. It is a system under strain.

Being Straight About The Record

It is worth being fair here, because credibility depends on it. The longest waits had genuinely been falling for the best part of a year before this. Anyone claiming the NHS has simply deteriorated in a straight line is not describing reality, and readers deserve better than that.

But that honesty cuts both ways. If ministers are entitled to claim credit for the months when the figures improved, they own the months when the figures turn. You do not get to describe the same statistical series as vindication on the way down and as an unforeseeable pressure on the way up.

The Royal College points to growing demand and additional pressure on services through the early summer. That is a reasonable explanation. It is not, however, an excuse, because demand pressure in an ageing population is the single most predictable feature of British healthcare. A health service that is repeatedly surprised by it is not being managed; it is being reacted to.

The Number Behind The Number

There is a habit in health policy of treating the waiting list as a scoreboard — a single figure to be brandished or buried at each fiscal event. That framing does real damage, because the headline total and the long-wait total can move independently, and it is the long waits that carry most of the human cost.

A list can shrink overall while the people at the very back of it wait longer than ever. Those are the patients whose conditions are most complex, whose surgery is hardest to schedule, and who have the least capacity to chase, complain or navigate their way forward. They are the easiest cohort in the NHS to leave behind, precisely because they are the least visible in the headline statistic.

That is why the College's focus on the 52-week figure matters, and why it should carry more weight in Parliament than it will.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK's position is that the NHS needs its money reaching the operating theatre rather than the layer of administration that has grown up around it. Fewer managers, more clinicians, and capital spent on the theatres, beds and diagnostic capacity that actually clear a backlog.

It means using spare capacity wherever it exists to cut the longest waits, judged strictly on whether the patient is treated free at the point of use and treated sooner. It means holding trusts accountable for the 52-week figure specifically, not just the headline list, so that the people at the back of the queue cannot be quietly written off in the pursuit of a better top-line number.

And it means telling the truth about the trend in both directions. Ten months of improvement was real. So is this reversal. The test of a government is which of the two it chooses to talk about.

Source: Royal College of Surgeons of England, Progress on longest NHS waits stalls as year-long waits rise by more than 11,000, August 2026, rcseng.ac.uk. Figures are RCS analysis of NHS England Referral to Treatment data to June 2026.
