Ofwat Just Waved Through Another £3.4 Billion for the Water Companies. Guess Who Pays.

Ofwat has provisionally approved £3.39 billion of extra funding that 13 water companies asked for, with customers of five suppliers set to see annual bills climb by up to £43 between 2027 and 2030. Over a billion of it goes on safeguarding assets customers already paid for. The consultation closes at 5pm on 24 September — and almost nobody has been told.

Here we go again. The regulator that is supposed to be on your side has just provisionally waved through £3.39 billion of extra funding that 13 water companies asked for — and the bill lands, as it always does, on the customer.

Ofwat calls it the "Cost Change Process". It is a mechanism that lets water companies top up their money between the full five-yearly price reviews, so they don't have to wait until 2029. In plain English: a mid-term pay rise, granted outside the process that was meant to hold them to account.

What It Actually Costs You

Customers of five suppliers are set to see annual bills climb by up to £43 between 2027 and 2030. That is on top of what has already happened. Average household water bills in England and Wales went up 27% in 2025-26, and a further 5.4% — about £33 — in 2026-27.

Add that to an energy price cap that rises again this month and you have the same story playing out in every regulated utility in this country: the customer is the last consideration and the first to pay.

Paying to Fix What Should Never Have Broken

Look at where the money is going. Of the £3.39bn, £1.2 billion is to "safeguard existing services and assets for future generations". £477 million is for growth pressures — unlocking housebuilding and data centre development. £34 million is for managing PFAS and other so-called forever chemicals.

Some of that is genuinely necessary work. Nobody sensible objects to cleaning chemicals out of drinking water. But read that first line again. Safeguarding existing assets. Billions of pounds to stop pipes and treatment works that customers already paid for from falling to bits.

My honest read is this: for thirty years these companies have paid out to shareholders, loaded themselves with debt, and deferred the boring, unglamorous maintenance. Now the bill for that neglect has come due — and the regulator's answer is to send it to you rather than to them. That is not investment. That is a bailout with a nicer name.

The Consultation Nobody Told You About

Here is the part that should make you angry. The draft determinations are open for public consultation until 5pm on 24 September 2026, with the final decision due in December. Ask yourself honestly: had you heard? Did anyone knock on your door about it? Was it on the news?

Let's be honest — this is how the establishment does difficult things. Announce it in the technical language of a regulator, open a "consultation" that only the industry's own lawyers will answer, close it three weeks later, and confirm it quietly in December when the country is thinking about Christmas. By the time it reaches your direct debit in 2027 it is a fact of life that nobody remembers agreeing to.

Where Reform UK Stands

A regulator that keeps approving what the companies ask for is not a regulator. It is a rubber stamp. Reform UK's position is straightforward: the people who ran these firms into the ground should carry the cost of putting them right, not the family already choosing between the heating and the shop. That means real consequences for failure instead of another mid-cycle top-up, dividends and bonuses restrained until the leaks, the sewage and the crumbling infrastructure are actually fixed, and consultations that are advertised to the public who pay rather than buried on a regulator's website for three weeks in September.

Water is not a luxury. You cannot shop around for it, you cannot switch supplier, and you cannot go without. When a monopoly fails, the answer is not to keep handing it money and hoping. It is to make failure hurt the people responsible for it. Until that changes, expect to be back here in 2029 reading the same story with a bigger number attached.
