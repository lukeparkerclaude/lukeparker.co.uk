Petrol Is the Highest Since November 2022. This Time Nobody Called It a Crisis.

ONS figures show petrol rose 9.1p a litre in a single month to 161.3p — the highest since November 2022. Diesel jumped 14.2p to 181.8p. Motor fuel inflation ran at 23.0% over the year. In 2022 numbers like these led the news for a fortnight. This time they were a sub-heading.

The Office for National Statistics published its August inflation figures last week. Most of the coverage went, reasonably enough, to the headline: CPI up to 3.1% from 2.9%.

Underneath it sat a set of numbers that should have had their own front page.

The Figures

The average price of petrol rose by 9.1 pence a litre between July and August. For comparison, over the same two months last year it rose by 0.3 pence. That took petrol to 161.3 pence a litre — the highest, the ONS says plainly, since November 2022.

Diesel did worse. Up 14.2 pence a litre in a month, against 0.8 pence over the same stretch in 2025, reaching 181.8 pence.

Across the year, motor fuel prices rose 23.0%, up from 15.5% the month before. The ONS names transport, and motor fuels in particular, as the largest single upward contribution to the inflation rate.

So when you hear that inflation "ticked up", understand what did the ticking. It was the pump.

The Tax Nobody Has to Vote For

Here is my honest read on why this landed so softly.

Fuel is the most regressive cost in British life, and the least visible to the people who set policy. If your work is within walking distance of a mainline station, a 9 pence rise is an irritation you notice once a month. If you are a carer driving between four visits in a morning, a sole trader with a van, a nurse on shifts that no bus timetable has ever accommodated, or anyone at all living in rural Lancashire, it is a pay cut. It is simply a pay cut that arrives without a letter, without a vote, and without anyone to complain to.

And it compounds. Diesel at 181.8p does not stay at the pump. It moves into the cost of everything that arrives on a lorry, which is very nearly everything. You will meet that 14.2 pence again on a shelf in a few months' time, wearing a different label.

In 2022, a petrol price at this level was a national emergency. There were front pages, emergency statements, a running count. The price is back and the volume is off. Not because it hurts less. Because the people it hurts most were never the ones setting the agenda.

Be Honest About the Cause

I am not going to pretend a minister reached over and pushed the price up. The driver here is the wholesale market, and that market is being shoved around by conflict in the Middle East. No British government controls that.

But governments do control three things, and ours keeps pretending otherwise.

They control the tax component, which remains the largest single element of what you hand over at the pump. They control how exposed we choose to be to imported energy rather than our own. And they control whether the burden of a global price shock is spread or simply left to land on whoever happens to need a vehicle to earn a living.

On all three, the answer from Westminster has been to look at the sky and say the weather is bad.

Where Reform UK Stands

Cut fuel duty, and mean it. Not a freeze announced with a fanfare and reversed in a Budget nobody reads. A cut, held, so that the people who drive for a living can plan further ahead than the next tank.

Produce more of our own energy, so that a dispute thousands of miles away does not automatically arrive as a 14 pence jump on a Lancashire forecourt.

And stop designing policy for people who do not need a car. The assumption running underneath a great deal of transport and net zero policy is that driving is a lifestyle choice to be gently discouraged. For millions of people, it is how the shift gets covered and the children get collected. Policy written by people with a Tube map in their heads will keep failing everyone who lives past the end of the line.

161.3 pence. Highest since November 2022. It deserved more than a sub-heading.

Sources: Office for National Statistics, "Consumer price inflation, UK: August 2026" (ons.gov.uk). Petrol up 9.1 pence per litre month-on-month to an average 161.3 pence, described by the ONS as the highest since November 2022; diesel up 14.2 pence to 181.8 pence; motor fuel prices up 23.0% in the 12 months to August 2026 against 15.5% the previous month; transport, particularly motor fuels, the largest upward contribution to the annual CPI rate, which rose to 3.1% from 2.9%.
