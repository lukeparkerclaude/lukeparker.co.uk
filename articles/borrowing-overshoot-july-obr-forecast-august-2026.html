Borrowing Overshoots Again: £1.8bn in July When the OBR Forecast a Surplus

July borrowing came in at £1.8bn. The OBR had forecast a £500m surplus. With national debt at 94.1% of GDP and a Budget due on 28 October, the numbers are closing in on the Chancellor.

There is a moment in every government's life when the spreadsheet stops cooperating. For John Healey, three weeks into the job of Chancellor, that moment arrived at seven o'clock this morning. The Office for National Statistics reported that the government borrowed £1.8 billion in July. The Office for Budget Responsibility had forecast a £500 million surplus. Most City economists had pencilled in zero. Every one of them was wrong in the same direction.

A Miss, Not a Rounding Error

July is normally a good month for the Exchequer. Self-assessment tax receipts land, and the public finances tend to look their healthiest. That is precisely why the OBR expected the books to be in surplus. Instead borrowing was £1.8 billion, some £700 million higher than in July last year — an increase of 68.7%.

ONS chief economist Grant Fitzner explained it plainly: "Borrowing was slightly higher this month than in July last year, with spending growth outpacing higher receipts, including from self-assessed taxes which often feed in more strongly in July." Spending is outrunning revenue in the month that is supposed to flatter the figures.

The Tax Take Is at a Record and It Still Is Not Enough

Here is the number that ought to end the argument about whether Britain is undertaxed. Income tax receipts in July hit a record £17.1 billion, up £1.7 billion on the same month last year. A record haul from working people, and the government still could not balance a single month that the forecasters expected to be in the black.

This is what fiscal drag looks like in practice. Income tax and National Insurance thresholds are frozen until April 2031. Every pay rise pulls a few more people over a line that has not moved, and the Treasury collects more without a minister ever having to stand up and announce a tax rise. It is a stealth tax, it is working exactly as designed, and it is still not covering the bills.

Debt interest alone came to £7.7 billion in July, up £700 million on the year. Spending on social benefits rose by around £2 billion. Public sector net debt now stands at £2.985 trillion, or 94.1% of GDP — a whisker below the £3 trillion mark.

The Part the Government Will Point To

In fairness, and it matters that this is said: borrowing across the financial year so far is down. For April to July 2026 the total was £56.7 billion, which is £6.0 billion — 9.6% — lower than the same four months last year. Anyone telling you borrowing is simply spiralling upward is not reading the release.

The Chancellor made that point himself, saying fiscal discipline is "the bedrock of our UK economic stability and national security" and that the government is "cutting the deficit faster than any other G7 economy."

But the year-to-date figure is still running above what the OBR forecast. That is the bit that counts. It is not enough to borrow less than last year if you are borrowing more than you promised. The fiscal rules are measured against the forecast, not against nostalgia.

Eleven Weeks to the Budget

Healey has confirmed his first Budget for Wednesday 28 October, promising a Budget that "moves money and power out of Westminster, and into every postcode around Britain", built on "fiscal discipline" and meeting the fiscal rules. Those are three commitments that are going to have to be reconciled with each other, and today's figures made that job harder.

The government has already announced a VAT cut on electricity costing around £850 million this year and a business rates cut for pubs and clubs worth about £100 million a year from 2027. Both are welcome. Neither is free. And with the tax burden already producing record receipts, there is no comfortable room left on the revenue side.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK would start from the other end of the ledger. Before asking working people for another penny, government should be able to explain what it is doing with the pennies it already has. That means scrapping programmes that deliver nothing — this government cancelled a £1.8 billion digital ID scheme and immediately found the money for an energy tax cut, which rather proves the point — cutting the consultancy bill, and unfreezing the tax thresholds that are quietly dragging ordinary earners into higher bands.

A country with a record income tax take and a £2.985 trillion debt does not have a revenue problem. It has a spending discipline problem. On 28 October we will find out whether the new Chancellor agrees.
