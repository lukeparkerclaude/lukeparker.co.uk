Water Firms Still Mark Their Own Homework. That's the Scandal Nobody Fixed.

Since 2009, the way we check whether a water company is polluting our rivers has largely been to ask the water company to check itself. On Friday that scheme landed in the middle of a criminal courtroom. The charges are unproven and it is for a jury to decide them — but you do not need a verdict to see that a system where the regulated collect their own evidence, and 'no sample' counts as a pass, was badly built from day one.

Since 2009, the way we check whether a water company is polluting our rivers has largely been this: we ask the water company to check itself.

That is not a conspiracy theory. It is the official scheme, it is called operator self-monitoring, and on Friday it landed squarely in the middle of a criminal courtroom.

What Happened on Friday — and What Has Not Been Decided

Let me set this out carefully, because it matters that I get it right.

Matthew Wright, 60, the former chief executive of Southern Water, appeared at Bromley Magistrates' Court on Friday alongside six other former employees — Philip Barker, 57; Clive Massey, 64; Mark Gregory, 63; Mark Butler, 45; David James, 58; and Terry Stephens, 67 — to face a charge of conspiracy to defraud the Environment Agency and Ofwat.

The allegation is that between January 2012 and December 2017 the self-monitoring regime was manipulated through artificial "no-flow events" at wastewater treatment works, in order to avoid failing environmental checks. Prosecutors say the effect could have been to avoid fines of £45 million or higher. Separately, Southern Water as a company has been charged with dozens of offences of failing to comply with, or contravening, an environmental permit condition.

District Judge Stephen Leake found the charges related and sent the case to Maidstone Crown Court, where the defendants are due to appear on 9 October. All seven were granted unconditional bail.

Now the part I will not skate over. These are allegations. Nothing has been proven. Mr Wright denies any wrongdoing and, his solicitor has said, cooperated with the Environment Agency's investigation. Whether these charges are made out is a matter for a jury, not for me and not for a website. I express no view on the guilt or innocence of any defendant, and neither should anyone else until the trial is done. That is not me being mealy-mouthed — it is how a fair trial works, and I want the process respected.

The Bit That Should Alarm You Whatever the Verdict

Here is my honest read, and it is about the rules rather than the people.

Set the case aside entirely. What the last week has put in front of the public is the design of the system itself, and the design is indefensible on its own terms.

A wastewater treatment works can only operate with an environmental permit from the Environment Agency. Since 2009, the treated effluent coming out of those works has been tested under operator self-monitoring — meaning the water companies do the testing. There are safeguards: the visits are unannounced, and the sampling teams are meant to operate separately from the teams running the works. The results then go to the Environment Agency and Ofwat, and penalties follow if the permit terms aren't met.

And then there is this. If the discharge flow at the moment of testing is too low to take a sample at all — a "no-flow event" — it is not recorded as a fail.

Think about what that means as a matter of pure system design, with nobody accused of anything. You have a regulated industry that collects the evidence used to judge it, and a rule stating that an absence of evidence counts as a pass. Any first-year auditor would tell you that is a control failure. It is the regulatory equivalent of letting a pupil mark his own homework and treating a blank page as full marks.

You do not need a verdict to call that a bad system. It was a bad system on the day it was written.

Meanwhile, Your Bill

And while all of this plays out, the direction of your bill is only ever one way.

This month Ofwat provisionally approved £3.4 billion of further funding for 13 water companies — companies had asked for £4.3 billion, and of the £3.533 billion Ofwat judged in scope, 96%, or £3.390 billion, was allowed in the draft determinations. Five of the 13 have been allowed to recover their funding through customer bills between 2027 and 2030. The consultation closes at 5pm on 24 September 2026, with final determinations due in December.

So the sequence, from where the customer is standing, runs like this: a monitoring regime the companies largely administer themselves, enforcement action that takes the best part of a decade to reach a courtroom, and a bill that goes up again regardless.

Where Reform UK Stands

Self-monitoring has to end. Testing that decides whether a company is fined should be done by, or directly commissioned by, the regulator — independently, at the company's expense, with the results published in full. A "no-flow event" should be logged, investigated and disclosed, not silently treated as a clean sheet. And no water company should be handed another penny on the bill for environmental improvements until the public can verify, from evidence it did not have to take on trust, that the last lot of money actually cleaned anything up.

People in this country are not unreasonable. They will pay for water that is safe and rivers that aren't full of sewage. What they will not forgive is being asked to pay more into a system that was never built to tell them the truth in the first place.
