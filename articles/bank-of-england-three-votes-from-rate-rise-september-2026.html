Three Votes From a Rate Rise — and a Budget That Wants More From You Anyway

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on Wednesday. Read past the headline and it was a 6-3 split, with three members voting to raise it to 4%. Inflation is back up to 3.1% and the Bank says it is likely to rise further over the coming quarters. The energy cap goes up on 1 October. The Budget lands on 28 October. Let's be honest about what that combination does to a household in Lancashire.

The headline on Thursday morning was reassuring: rates held. No change. Nothing to see here.

Read the actual minutes and it is a good deal less comfortable than that.

What the Bank Actually Decided

At its meeting ending on 16 September 2026, the Monetary Policy Committee voted by a majority of 6-3 to maintain Bank Rate at 3.75%. The three in the minority did not want a cut. They wanted an increase of 0.25 percentage points, to 4%.

That is the part worth sitting with. A third of the committee that sets the price of every mortgage, every overdraft and every business loan in this country thinks borrowing needs to get more expensive, not less.

Their reasoning is not hard to follow. CPI inflation rose to 3.1% in August, and the Bank's own summary says it is likely to rise further over coming quarters. The Bank also points to protracted conflict in the Middle East pushing crude and refined energy prices higher since its previous meeting, and notes those prices remain volatile and above pre-conflict levels.

Markets had largely expected the hold. What they are now pricing, according to reporting around the decision, is a rise of at least a quarter point when the committee meets again in November. That is a market expectation, not a promise — but it is the direction everyone with money on the table is betting.

Let's Be Honest About the Bank's Position

I am not going to pretend the Monetary Policy Committee is the villain here, because it isn't.

A central bank with an inflation target and inflation at 3.1% and climbing has a narrow set of options, and none of them are pleasant. Some of the pressure is genuinely imported: a war thousands of miles away is showing up in the price of a tank of diesel on the A59, and no Chancellor of any party can legislate that away. Holding rates rather than raising them was, on the face of it, the cautious call.

And 3.75% is not 15%. I am old enough to know the difference, and so are plenty of you.

But here is the bit that should bother everybody. Monetary policy is the only lever in the building that is currently pointing the other way from everything else — and it is being asked to do the whole job on its own.

The Squeeze Nobody Voted For

Line it up in order, because the calendar is brutal.

On 1 October, Ofgem's price cap rises 4% to £1,723 a year for a typical dual-fuel household — around £60 more, with the gas element alone up 8%. On 28 October, the Chancellor, John Healey, delivers a Budget that Andy Burnham has already declined to rule out tax rises in, saying he “won't be unrealistic” about the state of the public finances. And in November, on current market expectations, the Bank may well put the cost of your mortgage up.

Higher bills in October. Higher taxes in October. Higher borrowing costs in November. That is three squeezes on the same wage packet inside six weeks.

My honest read: a government that knows inflation is rising and knows the Bank is one vote-swing away from a hike has decided the right response is to take more money out of the economy. Whatever that is, it is not a plan for growth. It is a plan for a spreadsheet.

And remember how we got a Prime Minister and a Chancellor making these calls. Not one voter has been asked. The general election that produced this Parliament was fought in July 2024 on a prospectus, and under a leader, that no longer exist.

Where Reform UK Stands

You cannot squeeze a country into prosperity. When the central bank is tightening because prices are rising, the last thing a Treasury should do is pile tax rises on top of it. That is two hands on the same throat.

The honest answer starts with energy, because energy is the price underneath every other price. Strip the levies and net-zero costs loaded onto bills and you take pressure off inflation, off manufacturing, and off the heating in your front room all at once — which does more for the Bank's target than any amount of stern language from Threadneedle Street.

Then get the tax burden off work and off the firms that create it, so growth has somewhere to come from other than a forecast.

Three members of that committee just told you where they think this is heading. On 28 October, watch whether the Treasury listened — or whether it simply sends you the bill and calls it a tough choice. It isn't a tough choice. It is a choice, made by people this country has never had the chance to vote for or against.
