The Watchdog Is Reviewing Election Spending Limits — Here's the Honest Version

Vijay Rangarajan, chief executive of the Electoral Commission, has confirmed a review of the whole campaign spending framework — limits, party-versus-candidate spending, and reporting. It arrives weeks after two donors gave Reform UK £36 million each. I could write you the easy column about that timing. I'd rather write you the honest one, because the honest one is actually a stronger argument.

I could write you the easy column here. It writes itself, frankly.

Two donors give Reform UK £36 million each. Weeks later, the elections watchdog announces it is reviewing the rules on how much parties are allowed to spend. Cue outrage, cue the word "stitch-up", cue a thousand retweets.

The trouble is that the easy column would not be entirely straight with you, and I would rather be straight with you.

What Was Actually Announced

Vijay Rangarajan, chief executive of the Electoral Commission, told the Sunday Times that a review of the campaign spending framework is needed to keep the rules "fit for purpose" and to maintain public confidence in a level playing field. His stated reason is that political campaigning has "changed significantly in recent years", with a "significant amount" now being spent between election periods rather than in the regulated campaign window.

The review is broad. It covers spending limits themselves, the interaction between party spending and candidate spending, and the reporting requirements that sit underneath it all.

The number at the centre of it: at present, a party standing candidates in every constituency across Britain has a spending limit of £34 million in the year before a general election.

And the context nobody is pretending doesn't exist: earlier this month Ben Delo and Christopher Harborne each gave Reform UK £36 million — £72 million between them, reported as the largest political donations in British history.

The Honest Bit

Here is the part my own side will like least, and I am going to say it anyway, because credibility is built out of the inconvenient sentences.

Ministers first asked for this independent review back in July. Before the £72 million. Before anybody knew those cheques were coming.

So the review is not something conjured up in a panic the week after Reform banked the money. Anybody telling you otherwise is selling you a story rather than a fact, and I am not going to do that. There is a genuine, long-standing problem with spending that happens outside the regulated period — digital advertising, permanent campaigning, year-round operations — and it is not unreasonable for a regulator to look at it.

So What Is the Argument, Then?

The argument is not about whether the rules should be examined. It is about what the examination concludes, and who it happens to bind.

A spending framework designed when campaigning meant leaflets and battle buses is plainly out of date. Fine. But there are two ways to modernise it. One is to update the rules so that all the money — whenever it is spent, by whoever, through whatever vehicle — is visible and counted. The other is to look at the number, notice that a challenger party can now afford to reach the limit for the first time in its life, and quietly lower the ceiling.

The first is regulation. The second is incumbency protection wearing a hi-vis jacket.

And the test is simple: does whatever comes out of this review apply with exactly the same force to trade union political funds, to third-party campaign groups, to the vast year-round machinery of the established parties, and to the sympathetic outriders who spend heavily without ever appearing on a party's return? If yes, it is a reform. If the tightening lands hardest on the newest entrant, everyone will be able to see what it is.

My honest read: Rangarajan's stated reasoning is sound. What I will be watching is the number. If the review's headline recommendation turns out to be "the £34 million limit should come down", I would want to hear a very good explanation of why the correct moment to shrink what parties may spend talking to voters is the exact moment one party finally has something to spend.

Where Reform UK Stands

Transparency without limit. Limits without favouritism.

Publish every pound, from every source, fast — and extend that to the money currently spent outside the regulated period by everybody, not just political parties. If Parliament and the Commission then want to set spending limits, set them symmetrically: parties, unions, companies, campaign groups, all inside the same fence, all reported the same way.

What we will not accept is a rulebook rewritten to make it harder to be heard. The establishment spent decades comfortable with the spending rules while the money flowed its way. If the rules are genuinely broken, mend them properly — and mend them for everyone. Do it any other way and you will not be protecting public confidence in elections. You will be spending it.
