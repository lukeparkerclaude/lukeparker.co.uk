94,000 Fewer Payslips — and a Budget Coming That Wants More From You

There are 94,000 fewer people on a payroll than a year ago. Unemployment has climbed to 4.9%, with 1.77 million people out of work — 88,000 more than last year. Inflation has turned back upwards. The energy price cap goes up on 1 October. And on 28 October, a Prime Minister nobody elected delivers a Budget he has already hinted will raise taxes. Let's be honest about where that leads.

Politics is usually argued in adjectives. So let's do it in numbers instead, because the numbers are doing all the talking at the moment and none of it is good.

What the Figures Actually Say

The early estimate for July 2026 puts the number of payrolled employees 94,000 lower than a year earlier — a fall of 0.3% — at 30.3 million. That is not a one-month blip: payrolled employee numbers have been generally falling for around two years now, and they fell again over the three months to June.

Unemployment is 4.9%, with 1.77 million people aged 16 and over out of work. That is 88,000 more than a year ago, and the rate is up from 4.7%. Meanwhile the employment rate for people aged 16 to 64 has slipped to 75.1%, down from 75.3% a year earlier.

And prices have started climbing again. CPI inflation was 2.9% in the 12 months to July, up from 2.6% in June. The August figure lands on Wednesday. From 1 October, Ofgem's price cap rises 4% to £1,723 for a typical household using both gas and electricity — about £60 more a year, with the gas element alone up 8%.

Fewer payslips. More people out of work. Prices turning back up. Bills up on the first of the month.

Let's Be Honest About the Context

I am not going to stand here and tell you this is 1981, because it isn't, and you would rightly stop reading.

Unemployment at 4.9% is not a catastrophe by the standards of the last fifty years. Inflation at 2.9% is nothing like the shock of 2022. Some of the pressure on gas prices is genuinely global and not made in Westminster — Ofgem itself points to volatile wholesale gas markets. Around 11 million households, roughly 35% of them, are on fixed tariffs and will not feel October's rise at all. And the economy is growing: GDP rose 0.4% in July, after 0.3% in June, and is 1.6% up on the year. Straight talking means saying all of that out loud.

But hold two of those facts side by side, because this is the bit that should bother everybody. The economy grew, and the number of people on a payroll fell. Growth that does not show up in payslips is not a recovery anybody in Lancashire can spend.

But direction of travel is the whole ball game in economics. Every one of those indicators is pointing the wrong way at the same time, and they have been for months. That is not weather. That is climate.

And Now the Budget

Into that, on Wednesday 28 October — the date the Chancellor, John Healey, confirmed back on 31 July — lands the Budget.

Andy Burnham has already signalled which way he is going. He has hinted at tax rises, saying he "won't be unrealistic" about the state of the public finances while insisting his spending packages will be fully funded. You do not need a decoder ring for that sentence.

So here is my honest read. A government facing a shrinking payroll and a rising dole queue has decided the answer is to take more money out of the economy that employs people. When the cost of hiring somebody goes up, employers hire fewer somebodies. That is not ideology, it is arithmetic, and the 94,000 missing payslips are the receipt.

And remember who is doing it. Not one voter has been asked whether they want this Prime Minister, this Chancellor, or this Budget. The last general election was fought in July 2024 on a prospectus, and by a leader, that no longer exist.

Where Reform UK Stands

You do not tax your way out of a jobs problem. If payrolls are shrinking, the job of a Budget is to make hiring cheaper and working pay more — not to raise the cost of employing people and call it responsibility.

That means lifting the tax burden off work and off the businesses that create it, rather than squeezing harder because the spreadsheet says so. It means stripping the levies and net-zero costs loaded onto energy bills, because cheap energy is the foundation everything else is built on — factories, shops, and the heating in your front room. And it means a government honest enough to admit that after two years of falling payrolls, the problem might be the policy.

On 28 October, watch whose bill goes up. My bet is that it will be yours, and that the people presenting it will describe it as a tough choice they were forced into. It isn't. It is a choice — made by people the country has never had the chance to vote for or against.
