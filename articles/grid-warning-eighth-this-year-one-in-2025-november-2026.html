Eight Grid Warnings This Year. There Was One in All of 2025.

Yesterday the grid operator told the market it wanted a bigger safety cushion for the evening peak. It is the eighth such warning this year, against one in the whole of 2025. Nobody’s lights went out. But this is a warning light, and Britain should take it seriously.

Yesterday, Tuesday 24 November, the National Energy System Operator (NESO) issued an Electricity Margin Notice, a signal to the market that it wanted a bigger safety cushion between demand and available supply. The window was first set at 1pm to 8pm and later narrowed to 3pm to 8pm, and the shortfall against NESO’s preferred margin was put at 1,150MW, later revised to 800MW.

Let me be fair, because accuracy matters more than scare stories. NESO itself said the notice did not mean blackouts were imminent, and it was cancelled that afternoon. Nobody’s lights went out.

But Look at the Trend

According to The Telegraph, this was the eighth such warning so far this year, compared with just one in all of 2025. They have come in June, July, August, September, October and now twice in November. One warning is a blip. Eight is a pattern.

NESO pointed partly to “system constraints”. The Telegraph says that probably means grid bottlenecks, with power generated in Scotland unable to reach high-demand areas further south. Independent energy analyst Kathryn Porter linked it to maintenance and upgrade work on north-south lines, and called the warning “curious” because the market didn’t “look tight”.

And Who Pays?

A grid that cannot move its own power to where people need it does not run for free. The Telegraph reports NESO paid nearly £50m in a single day last month on balancing the system, and has sought a £1bn loan from the Treasury. Let’s be honest: one way or another, that lands on our bills or on the taxpayer.

Where Reform UK Stands

Energy security comes first. Families in Lancashire want two things from the energy system: that the lights stay on, and that the bill does not frighten them. I want reliable power that is there when the wind drops and the evenings are cold, a grid built to carry it, and an end to policies that pile costs onto households before the basics are secure. Eight warnings in a year should be a wake-up call, not a statistic.
