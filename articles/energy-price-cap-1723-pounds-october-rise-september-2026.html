Your Energy Bill Goes Up Again on 1 October. Nobody in Westminster Will Say Why.

Ofgem confirmed on 26 August that the energy price cap rises 4% from 1 October, taking the typical dual-fuel household from £1,663 to £1,723 a year. That is £60 more, with the gas element alone up 8%. It arrives just as the heating goes on. And not one person responsible for the policy choices behind it will stand up and own them.

Ofgem confirmed it on 26 August. From 1 October, the energy price cap rises by 4%. For a typical household using both gas and electricity, that takes the annual figure from £1,663 to £1,723 — an extra £60 a year, or about £5 a month.

Sixty pounds. I can already hear the response from the comfortable end of politics: it's only a fiver a month. Let's be honest about who says that, and who doesn't. Nobody who has ever sat with a pre-payment meter and a calculator says that.

Look at Where the Rise Actually Sits

The detail matters here, and it is worth being straight about it. Most of this increase is driven by gas, where bills go up around 8%. Households that do not use gas see a much smaller rise. Electricity is broadly stable.

So this is not a blanket hit — it is a targeted one, and it targets the homes that heat with gas. That is most of the country, and it is disproportionately older housing stock, older residents, and the parts of the North that were never going to be first in the queue for a heat pump.

The Cap Was Never a Cap on Cost

Around 22 million households on default tariffs are covered by the cap, and roughly a third of homes are on fixes and will not feel this particular rise. But here is the thing people misunderstand about the price cap: it is a cap on what a supplier may charge per unit, not a cap on what your life costs. It has now moved up so many times that calling it a cap is close to a joke at the bill-payer's expense.

Where Reform UK Stands

My honest read is that this is what a decade of energy policy looks like when it finally lands on a doormat. We chose to become more dependent on imported gas at world prices while loading domestic bills with the cost of a transition nobody was ever asked to vote on. Then ministers act surprised when the wholesale market moves and the public pays.

Reform UK's position is straightforward. Strip the policy levies off household energy bills and stop using the electricity bill as a stealth tax collector. Get serious about domestic supply — our own gas, in our own waters, taxed in our own Treasury, rather than shipped in and marked up. And be honest with people about the trade-offs instead of hiding them in a standing charge. If a policy is worth having, it is worth defending in public. If it can only survive by being buried on page two of a bill in October, it is not worth having.

Sources: Ofgem, "Energy price cap will rise by 4% from October 2026" and "Changes to energy price cap between 1 October and 31 December 2026", published 26 August 2026 (ofgem.gov.uk); Ofgem figures on the number of households on default tariffs protected by the cap.
