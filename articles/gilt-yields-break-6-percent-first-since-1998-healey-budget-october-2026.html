Gilts Above 6% for the First Time Since 1998 — and Healey's Budget Is Three Weeks Away

On 1 October the 30-year gilt yield briefly hit 6.04%, the first time above 6% since 1998. Analysts say it could wipe around £9bn off the Chancellor's headroom just weeks before the Budget. Let's be honest about what that means for you.

There are numbers in the economy that most people never think about, until one day they land on your doorstep as a bill. This week's number is 6%.

What Actually Happened

On Thursday 1 October the yield on the UK's 30-year government bond briefly hit 6.04% in early trading. That is the first time it has been above 6% since 1998. According to the FX market reports, it moved back above 6% again on 7 October. In plain English: the price the government pays to borrow money for three decades just hit a level we haven't seen since the last century.

In fairness, this was not a purely British problem. City AM reports that UK bonds were among the worst performers in a global bond sell-off that day, with oil above $100 a barrel as Middle East talks stalled. But UK yields rose by more than those in the US, Japan and Germany. Pantheon Macroeconomics put it bluntly, arguing that the wider gap over other countries is “fair given the UK's dire fiscal position”.

What It Means for the Budget

The Office for Budget Responsibility forecast in the spring that debt interest would cost £134.7 billion. Economists estimate that every one-point rise in rates adds about £15 billion to costs by 2031. City AM reports that analysis by RSM and other City firms suggests the jump could knock around £9 billion off the Chancellor's fiscal headroom, which stood at £23.6 billion in March.

That is before John Healey funds the pledges the Prime Minister has already made. And the Budget is on 28 October.

What Experts Are Saying

Andy Haldane, who sat on the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee until 2021, said: “we are skating on pretty thin ice in fiscal terms.” He added: “We have yet to balance the books this century.” MPA reports that lenders have already pulled residential mortgage deals priced below 5% as yields surged.

And there is pressure the other way too. Bank of England policymaker Mann has argued the Bank should raise Bank Rate from 3.75%, saying “UK financial conditions aren't tight enough”. Markets are pricing in hikes.

Let's Be Honest

My honest read is simple. When borrowing costs rise, there are only three places the money can come from: higher taxes, deeper cuts, or more borrowing at even higher rates. The Prime Minister has refused to rule out tax rises. You do not need to be an economist to see where this ends up if nothing changes: with working people paying the interest on a state that does not know how to stop spending.

Where Reform UK Stands

Britain cannot tax its way out of a borrowing problem it created by spending too much on the wrong things. Reform UK would go after waste in Whitehall, stop wasting billions on things the public never asked for, and back growth and the people who create it. Credibility with the bond markets comes from discipline, not from hoping the problem goes away before the next election.
