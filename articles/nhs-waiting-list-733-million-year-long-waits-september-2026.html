7.33 Million on the Waiting List. 111,000 Waiting Over a Year. And It's Going Back Up.

The latest referral-to-treatment figures for July put the England waiting list at 7.33 million cases — around 6.21 million individual people. Roughly 111,000 of them have now been waiting more than a year, up from about 106,000 the month before. The median wait is 12 weeks, against 7.3 weeks before the pandemic. These are not abstractions. Every one of them is somebody's mum in pain on a sofa.

The July referral-to-treatment figures put the England waiting list at 7.33 million cases — around 6.21 million individual people, some of whom are waiting for more than one thing. About 2.53 million of them have been waiting longer than the 18-week standard.

And the number that should stop a minister mid-sentence: roughly 111,000 people have now been waiting over a year. In June it was about 106,000. So it went up.

A Year Is Not a Wait. It Is a Life on Hold.

Let's be honest about what twelve months on a list actually means, because the word "waiting" does a lot of dishonest work. It means a hip that stops you walking the dog. It means a job you cannot hold down. It means a carer — usually a spouse, usually unpaid, often elderly themselves — doing a year of work the state is not doing. It means, in the worst cases, a condition that was operable in month two and is something else entirely by month eleven.

The median wait is now 12 weeks. Before the pandemic, in July 2019, it was 7.3 weeks. We have quietly accepted that the normal wait has nearly doubled and called it recovery.

The Front Door Is No Better

In August, 75% of people attending A&E were admitted, transferred or discharged within four hours — slightly worse than the month before. The standard is 95%. That is a quarter of everyone who walks through the door of an emergency department waiting longer than the NHS's own definition of acceptable, in August, before winter has laid a finger on it.

Where the Money Went Is a Fair Question

My honest read: nobody serious thinks the NHS is short of money in cash terms. It has had real increases year after year. What it is short of is anybody willing to ask what those increases bought. We have layer upon layer of management, a procurement system that would embarrass a corner shop, and a culture in which the response to a bad number is a new target rather than a new plan.

I want to be fair where fairness is due. The people doing the actual work — the nurses, the porters, the GPs running clinics past seven at night — are not the problem. They are carrying a system that is failing around them, and they are the only reason these figures are not worse.

Where Reform UK Stands

Reform UK's position is that the NHS needs reform, not reverence. Money should follow the patient to wherever the treatment can actually happen, and that means using every bit of spare capacity in the country rather than letting somebody rot on a list for a year on principle. Cut the administrative tier ruthlessly and put the saving into frontline treatment. Publish waiting times by hospital and by consultant, in plain English, so the public can see who is performing and who is not. And stop treating a rising year-long-wait figure as bad weather that has blown in. It is a result. Somebody chose it.
