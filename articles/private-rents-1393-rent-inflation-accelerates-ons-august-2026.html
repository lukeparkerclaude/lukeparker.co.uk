Private Rents Climb To £1,393 As Rent Inflation Speeds Up Again

Official figures show the average British private rent has reached £1,393 — and the rate of increase is accelerating, not easing. For millions of working people, that is the whole story of this economy in one number.

There is a particular kind of letter that lands on a doormat at the end of the month. It is polite, it is professional, and it tells you your rent is going up again. You already knew. You just did not know by how much.

This week the Office for National Statistics put a number on it. The average private rent in the United Kingdom has reached £1,393 a month, up 3.7% over the twelve months to July 2026. That alone would be a hard enough headline. The more troubling detail is buried underneath: a month earlier, the annual figure was 3.3%. Rent inflation is not cooling. It is picking up speed again.

The Numbers Behind The Squeeze

Break the figures down by nation and the pressure is everywhere. In England, the average rent is now £1,451, up 3.8% on the year. In Wales it is £843, up 4.5%. In Scotland, £1,016, up 1.7%. Three different housing markets, three different sets of devolved decisions, and in each case the direction of travel for renters is the same.

Politicians like to talk about inflation as though it were weather — something that arrives, passes over, and eventually clears. Renters know better. A rent rise does not pass over. It becomes the new floor. Every subsequent increase is calculated from a higher base, which means even a "modest" percentage lands as a larger sum in real money, every single month, for as long as you hold the tenancy.

And this is a cost that cannot be deferred, switched, or shopped around. You can change energy supplier. You can buy a cheaper brand at the supermarket. You cannot negotiate with the roof over your head.

Where The Pressure Bites Hardest

The regional detail deserves more attention than it will get. Within England, rent inflation over the year to July 2026 was highest in the North East at 6.3%, and lowest in the South East at 2.9%.

Read that again, because it cuts against a lazy assumption that runs through a great deal of housing commentary. The sharpest increases are not in the expensive corners of the South. They are in the parts of the country with the lowest average wages and the thinnest margins — the places least able to absorb them. A 6.3% rise in the North East is not the same event as a 2.9% rise in the South East, whatever the spreadsheet says.

This is what makes the standard Westminster response so inadequate. National averages flatten exactly the differences that matter to the people living through them.

The Question Nobody In Government Wants Asked

Rents are set where supply meets demand. That is not ideology; it is arithmetic. If you want rents to stop climbing, you either build considerably more homes or you moderate the growth in the number of people competing for the ones that exist. Serious countries do both. Britain has spent two decades doing neither with any conviction.

Instead we get gestures. Consultations. Strategies with launch events and no foundations. Meanwhile the housebuilding numbers fall short of what is needed year after year, and demand keeps rising regardless of whether the homes are there to meet it.

You cannot run an immigration policy and a housing policy as if they were unrelated files in different departments. They are the same policy, viewed from two ends. Every serious analysis of housing demand accepts this. It remains close to unsayable in government.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK's approach starts from the position that housing is infrastructure, not an afterthought. Build on brownfield land first, with planning decisions taken in weeks rather than years, so that supply can actually respond to demand instead of trailing a decade behind it.

It means being honest that the level of net migration Britain has run in recent years places real, measurable pressure on the rental market, and that no housing policy which ignores that will succeed. It means backing the small landlords who provide much of the country's rental stock rather than taxing and regulating them out of the sector, because every landlord who exits takes homes off the market and pushes rents up for everyone left behind.

And it means measuring success in the only currency that matters to a renter in Preston, Blackburn or Burnley: whether the letter at the end of the month asks for more than it did last year.

On today's figures, the answer is still yes — and by a wider margin than last month.

Source: Office for National Statistics, Private rent and house prices, UK: August 2026 (data to July 2026), ons.gov.uk.
