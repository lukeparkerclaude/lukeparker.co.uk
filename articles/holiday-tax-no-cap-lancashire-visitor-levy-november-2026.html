A Holiday Tax With No Cap — And Lancashire Is in the Firing Line

MPs debate Labour's Visitor Levy Bill today. It lets mayors and strategic authorities tax every overnight stay, with no legal cap on the rate and no requirement to spend the money on tourism. For a county that relies on Blackpool and the coast, that is a tax on our own front door.

Today MPs hold the Second Reading of the Visitor Levy (England) Bill, introduced on 4 November. Call it what it is: a holiday tax. It would let mayors and other strategic authorities in England charge a percentage on top of the price of an overnight stay — hotels, B&Bs, holiday lets, the lot.

No Cap, No Guarantee

Two things in this Bill should stop any MP in their tracks. First, there is no legal cap on the rate. Each authority sets its own percentage. Ten mayors have voluntarily said they will stay at or below 5%, but a promise from a politician is not a limit in law.

Second, the Bill does not require the money to be spent on tourism or hospitality. It can go on the authority's wider functions. So the family paying the levy in a seaside guest house has no guarantee a penny of it goes back into the resort they are keeping afloat.

The industry has not minced its words. Allen Simpson, chief executive of UKHospitality, called it “genuinely shocking to see the government bring forward legislation to hike the cost of a family holiday”. Oxford Economics analysis of a 5% levy, cited by the sector, estimates it would cost 33,000 jobs, add £1.6 billion to holidaymakers' tax bills and cost the Treasury almost £700 million.

Why Lancashire Should Pay Attention

Local reporting earlier this year noted that, because of its new combined authority status, Lancashire would be the only area outside the mayoral areas able to bring in a tourism levy. That puts Blackpool, Morecambe, the Fylde coast and the Ribble Valley squarely in scope.

Blackpool's hoteliers have already given their verdict. Chris Rinder of the Fossil Tree Hotel said it would be “totally bonkers to introduce it to a place like Blackpool”. Ian White of StayBlackpool called it “practically unworkable”. These are the people who actually run the businesses. Westminster should listen.

Let's Be Honest About Who Pays

A levy like this does not hit the wealthy weekender in a boutique hotel. It hits working families on a week by the sea, who have already been squeezed by everything else. My honest read: this is a new tax dressed up as “local empowerment”, and the lack of a cap tells you where it is heading.

Where Reform UK Stands

No new taxes on family holidays. Reform UK MPs have tabled a reasoned amendment opposing the Bill, citing industry warnings that a 5% levy could add around £100 to a two-week family holiday. As a Lancashire county councillor, I will oppose any attempt to bring a holiday tax to our coast. Our resorts need visitors, not another reason for them to stay away.
