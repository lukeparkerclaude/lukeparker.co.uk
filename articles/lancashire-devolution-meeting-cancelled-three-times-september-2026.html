Cancelled Three Times: Lancashire Waits While Whitehall Lectures Us on Devolution

Andy Burnham has built his whole pitch on devolution — No. 10 North, more power for mayors, the best civil servants seconded out of Whitehall. Meanwhile the leader of Lancashire County Council has tried three times since February to get a meeting with the minister responsible, and three times it has been cancelled. That is not a difference of opinion about policy. That is a county being kept waiting in the corridor.

There is a version of the devolution argument where everybody is terribly high-minded about subsidiarity and local voice and the beauty of decisions taken close to home. Then there is the version where you actually try to book the meeting.

County councillor Stephen Atkinson, the Reform leader of Lancashire County Council and chair of the Lancashire Combined County Authority, put the real version on the record at a Combined Authority meeting this month.

"We have tried three times [since February] to get a meeting with the minister about understanding what settlement there would be, and it's been cancelled three times. We'll be trying again, but until we have the facts in front of us I don't think it would be right for the people of Lancashire for us to agree to anything."

Three attempts. Three cancellations. Seven months.

The Minister Who Can't Find the Diary

The minister in question is Jim McMahon MP, restored to the Cabinet as Minister for Local Government, Devolution and Growth by Andy Burnham over the summer. This is the same government that has made devolution its signature tune — No. 10 North in Manchester, a National Economic Council, a pledge to second the best Whitehall civil servants out to mayoral authorities.

All of which is fine. Lovely, even. But a government that genuinely believed in pushing power out of London would not leave the second-largest county economy in the North of England holding on the line since February. Blog Preston asked the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government whether any meeting was planned. No response by the time they published.

Let's be honest about what that pattern actually communicates. It is not hostility. It is something worse: indifference. Lancashire simply is not near the top of anybody's list in Whitehall, and never has been.

What Everyone Else Is Getting

The Lancashire Business Board, chaired by Mo Isap OBE, has written to the Combined Authority pressing for a mayoral deal. The letter is backed by the likes of the University of Lancashire, the North and Western Lancashire Chamber of Commerce and the Preston Partnership — so this is not one man with a bee in his bonnet.

Their central figure is worth sitting with. Since South Yorkshire elected mayor Oliver Coppard, the letter says, he has secured an integrated settlement plus further earmarked funds totalling £2bn — which they put at around £1.4m a day for skills, transport, business support and housing.

Meanwhile Cheshire and Cumbria are set to have mayors in May 2027. Lancashire has a larger economy than both. And despite hosting one of the largest aerospace clusters anywhere in the world, Lancashire did not appear in the government's own Northern Growth Strategy.

Read that last sentence again. A Northern Growth Strategy. Without Lancashire in it.

Why Holding the Line Is the Right Call

Here is where I'll part company with some of the people shouting loudest for a mayor tomorrow.

Cllr Atkinson's position is not obstruction. It is the entirely reasonable proposition that you do not sign a constitutional change to how your county is governed before anyone has told you what you get for it. His deputy, county councillor Simon Evans, has made the point that the more recent devolution settlements are simply not as generous as the early ones — which is true, and which is exactly why the detail matters. Atkinson has also noted that Lancashire's growth rate has been running at a similar rate to the Liverpool City Region, which has one of the longest-established mayoral deals in the country.

My honest read: a mayor is not a magic wand. It is a negotiating position. And you do not improve a negotiating position by agreeing to the deal before you have seen the terms.

What makes it worse is the fog around everything else. Local government reorganisation has been paused over the threat of legal challenges elsewhere in the country, which means the shadow elections for new unitary councils will not now happen in May 2027 — instead there will be elections to the existing councils in Ribble Valley, South Ribble and Preston. Ribble Valley's Labour MP Maya Ellis has indicated a mayoral election in May 2028 is being eyed after her own conversations with the minister. So the MP can get a meeting. The council leader cannot.

And on the Combined Authority itself, the arithmetic is tight: four votes, with Blackpool and Blackburn with Darwen understood to favour a mayoral deal, the county council's two against as things stand, and Atkinson holding the casting vote as chair. This will be decided by people who live here, not by a press release from London.

Where Reform UK Stands

Show us the money, then we'll talk about the model. Reform UK in Lancashire is not against devolution. We are against signing blank cheques — which is, after all, the habit that got the country into this mess in the first place.

So: publish the settlement figure. Put the powers in writing. Tell the people of Lancashire what a mayor would actually control and what it would cost them in precept, and let them weigh it. If the offer is good, make the case in public and win the argument. If it is thin, say so honestly instead of dangling it and cancelling the meeting.

Lancashire is not a supplicant. We have the aerospace, the coast, the universities, the manufacturing base and the people who pay for all of it. We are not asking Whitehall for a favour. We are asking for a phone call to be honoured — and after seven months and three cancellations, that is not a lot to ask.
