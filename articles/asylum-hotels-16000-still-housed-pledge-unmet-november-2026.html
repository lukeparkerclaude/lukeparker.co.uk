16,000 Asylum Seekers Are Still in Hotels — Credit Where It’s Due, But the Pledge Is Still Unmet

The latest Home Office figures show 16,021 asylum seekers in hotels at the end of June, half the number of a year earlier. I’ll give credit where it’s due — the number has fallen. But the system still costs £4.36 billion a year, and the promise to end hotels is not yet kept.

I’m going to do something the political class rarely does: start with the good news, accurately. Because if we want people to trust us on the bad news, we have to be straight about both.

What the Figures Actually Say

Home Office statistics show that at the end of June 2026 there were 16,021 asylum seekers housed in hotels, down 50% from 32,041 a year earlier. It is the lowest figure since the data was first reported in 2022. The peak was 56,018 at the end of September 2023.

The wider picture has improved too. Asylum applications in the year to June were 85,891, down 21% on the year before, and the number waiting for an initial decision halved from 90,812 to 40,168. Total asylum spending in 2025-26 was £4.36 billion, down 8% from £4.76 billion, and below the £5.38 billion peak in 2023/24. That figure excludes the cost of intercepting small boats.

So: the direction is right. I’ll say that plainly.

But 16,000 People Is Still 16,000 People

Here is the other side of the same statistic. Sixteen thousand asylum seekers are still living in hotels paid for by the taxpayer, and the system is still costing more than £4 billion a year. Small boat arrivals made up 40% of people claiming asylum in the 12 months to June — 33,949 people. Those are the Home Office’s own numbers.

The Home Secretary, Shabana Mahmood, says the government is "restoring control at our border and fairness to the migration system" — and, to her credit, she admits there is still work to do to meet its pledge to end hotel use by the end of this Parliament. Notice what that pledge is: by the end of this Parliament. Not now. Not this year. Years away, with an election yet to be faced in between.

Why It Matters in Lancashire

As a county councillor I see how this lands in communities: stretched services, local anger, and people asking why it is the taxpayer who has to foot the bill. They deserve a government that sets a firm date and keeps to it — not one that moves the goalposts.

My honest read is this: falling numbers are welcome, but a fall from a record peak is not the same as fixing the problem. A system that has shrunk is still a system that is costing billions.

Where Reform UK Stands

End the hotels, secure the border, and put a date on it. Reform UK believes illegal arrivals should not be housed at the taxpayer’s expense, and that the way to end hotel use for good is to stop the boats and remove those with no right to be here. Fewer arrivals, faster decisions, proper removals — and a government held to a deadline that the public can see.

Source: Home Office asylum statistics to end of June 2026 as reported by ITV News, 27 August 2026: 16,021 asylum seekers in hotels (down 50% from 32,041; peak 56,018 in September 2023), applications 85,891 (down 21%), awaiting initial decision 40,168 (from 90,812), small boats 40% (33,949) of claims, asylum spending £4.36bn in 2025-26 (from £4.76bn; peak £5.38bn in 2023/24), Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood statement and end-of-Parliament pledge.
