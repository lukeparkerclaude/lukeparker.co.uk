77,000 People Were Allowed to Stay on Article 8 Last Year. Labour Reckons Its Bill Refuses 11,700.

In 2025 there were 77,000 grants of permission to remain in the UK under Article 8 family life or private life rules, about 7% of all visas granted in-country. Labour's Immigration and Asylum Bill, introduced on 30 June, is meant to tighten this. The Home Office impact assessment suggests it could produce 11,700 more refusals. That is one in seven. And the same assessment concedes more than half of those refused may simply remain here illegally anyway.

Let's start with a number almost nobody knows. In 2025, there were 77,000 grants of permission to remain in the United Kingdom under the Article 8 family life or private life rules. That is roughly 7% of all visas granted in-country that year. Of those, 42,000 went to first-time applicants. Another 500 people living abroad were granted Article 8 visas.

Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights protects the right to respect for private and family life. It is invoked by people who would otherwise have no right to stay, and it is decided case by case by tribunals whose reasoning most voters never see.

Now the number that goes with it. The Home Office's own impact assessment for the Immigration and Asylum Bill suggests that the Bill "could" produce 11,700 more Article 8 cases refused.

One in Seven

Hold those two figures side by side. Seventy-seven thousand grants. Eleven thousand seven hundred additional refusals, on a hedged estimate with no clear time period attached to it.

That is roughly one in seven. Six out of seven carry on exactly as before.

And it gets worse, because the same impact assessment concedes that more than half of people whose Article 8 case fails may nevertheless remain in the UK illegally. So of the 11,700 additional refusals, a good chunk are people who will be refused and then stay anyway. This is what a government policy looks like when it has been designed to produce a headline rather than an outcome.

What the Bill Actually Does

To be fair, and I will be, the Bill is not nothing. Its Part 2 genuinely tightens how Article 8 is applied.

It would narrow the definition of family life so it is normally limited to the core cohabiting family — a spouse, civil partner or partner, and a parent-child relationship where the child is under 18. It would require applicants to be, and be likely to remain, financially independent rather than simply financially independent today. It would add a mandatory good character consideration counting against anyone who has committed a criminal offence or failed to co-operate with immigration authorities. Private or family life formed while in the UK unlawfully would carry no weight rather than little weight. And for foreign national offenders, the current sentence-based tiers would be replaced by a general rule that judges can only block deportation in exceptional circumstances.

Those are real changes and I am not going to pretend they are cosmetic. But the House of Commons Library, in its briefing on the Bill, notes that the word "normally" keeps appearing — which preserves judicial flexibility — and that courts may decide the "no weight" rule does not always require them to ignore family life completely. It also flags that it is not clear how the revised rules for people sentenced to more than four years are actually stricter than what exists now.

The Bit That Settles It

Here is the sentence that tells you everything, straight from the Library briefing: the Bill "would not attempt to eliminate the ability of migrants to invoke Article 8". And the meaning of Article 8 is, in the end, determined not in Westminster but by the European Court of Human Rights in Strasbourg.

So Parliament can redraft the test as carefully as it likes. It is drafting inside a framework it does not control, to be interpreted by judges applying a convention it did not write.

One more figure from the impact assessment, because it is the whole argument in a line: 71% of people newly granted residence rights in 2025 had either entered the UK illegally or overstayed their previous visa.

Where Reform UK Stands

My honest read is that this Bill is an attempt to look tough on a system the government has already decided it will not fundamentally change. Tightening a test you do not ultimately control, to refuse one case in seven, half of whom stay anyway, is not border control. It is paperwork with a press release attached.

Reform UK's position is that Parliament, elected by the British people, should have the final say on who has the right to live in this country. That means a domestic framework where the rules are written here, applied here, and can be changed here when the public votes for change — not one where ministers spend a year drafting clauses and then wait to be told in Strasbourg what they were allowed to mean. Refusal should mean removal. Leave granted for a purpose should end when that purpose ends. And if seven in ten people newly granted the right to stay entered illegally or overstayed, the honest conclusion is that breaking the rules is currently a viable immigration strategy. Fix that, and you will not need an impact assessment to tell you it worked.

Figures as cited in that briefing: 77,000 Article 8 family life or private life grants in 2025 (around 7% of in-country visas), of which 42,000 first-time applicants, plus 500 granted abroad; the impact assessment "suggests that there could be 11,700 more Article 8 cases refused"; more than half of people whose Article 8 case fails may nevertheless remain in the UK illegally; and 71% of people newly granted residence rights in 2025 had either entered the UK illegally or overstayed a previous visa.
