A Record 7.27 Million A&E Visits — and Nearly 3,000 People a Day Treated in Corridors

Nearly three thousand people a day were treated in hospital corridors in England last month — in a setting the NHS itself classifies as not clinically appropriate or safe. A&E attendances hit a summer record of 7.27 million. The waiting list climbed to its highest in nine months, and a target to cut year-long waits below 1% has been missed by half again. Here are the numbers in full, including the ones that got better.

Nearly three thousand people a day were treated in hospital corridors in England last month. Not on trolleys in a side room. In corridors — a setting the NHS itself formally classifies as not clinically appropriate or safe.

That figure sits in the performance data NHS England published this week, and I want to walk through it properly, because it deserves more than a headline.

A Record Summer in A&E

Across June, July and August, a record 7.27 million people attended A&E departments in England. That is up from 7.03 million in summer 2025, and almost one million more than the summer of 2022, when the figure was 6.37 million.

August alone saw 2.34 million attendances — a record for that month. And 75.0% of patients were seen within four hours, down from 75.4% in July. The target is for every trust to reach 82% by March 2027.

Think about what that trajectory actually means. A million more people walking through the doors of emergency departments in a single summer than four years ago, into a system that was already stretched.

Corridor Care, by the Numbers

An average of 2,973 patients a day received corridor care in England in August: 2,296 in A&E departments and 677 elsewhere in hospitals. That is down slightly from an average of 3,028 a day in July.

We have only been able to see these numbers since May this year — four months of published data — and across that whole period the figure has sat stubbornly between roughly 2,900 and 3,200 a day.

The definition matters, so here it is. A patient counts if they have been receiving care for at least 45 minutes somewhere that is not a clinically appropriate and safe setting. The criteria include whether there is oxygen and a call bell at the bedside, whether privacy can be maintained, whether the patient can reach food, water and a toilet, and whether the lights can be turned off and the noise brought down enough for them to sleep.

Read that list again and picture your mother in it.

The Target They Quietly Missed

The waiting list for routine hospital treatment has risen to its highest level for nine months. An estimated 7.33 million treatments were waiting at the end of July, relating to 6.21 million patients — up from 7.27 million treatments and 6.15 million patients at the end of June.

The long waits are where it bites hardest. 111,379 people had been waiting more than a year to start treatment at the end of July, up from 105,711 a month earlier. Those waiting more than 65 weeks rose from 7,831 to 9,095. Those waiting more than 18 months rose from 1,099 to 1,494.

And here is the one that should be a headline in its own right. 1.52% of people on the list had been waiting more than 52 weeks in July, up from 1.45% in June. The Government and NHS England had set a target for that figure to be below 1% by March this year. It is now half as big again as the target, and moving in the wrong direction.

On the 18-week measure, 65.4% of patients began treatment within 18 weeks in July, down from 65.8% in June — though still above the 65% target set for March. The new target is 70% by March 2027, and the pledge is 92% by the end of this Parliament in 2029. From 65.4%, with the list rising, that is a very long way to travel.

Be Fair — Some of It Got Better

I'm not going to stand here and tell you everything is collapsing, because the data doesn't say that and you deserve the truth rather than the version that suits me.

Cancer performance improved. 79.3% of patients urgently referred for suspected cancer were diagnosed or had cancer ruled out within 28 days in July, up from 79.0% in June — still short of the 80% target, but moving the right way. The proportion beginning treatment within 62 days of an urgent referral rose to 71.2% from 69.9%.

Ambulance times improved too. The average response for the most urgent, life-threatening calls was seven minutes and 56 seconds in August, down from eight minutes in July, against a seven-minute standard. Category 2 calls — heart attacks, strokes, sepsis — averaged 28 minutes and 52 seconds, down from 29 minutes and 49 seconds. The year's running average is still 29 minutes against a 25-minute target, but the direction of travel in August was right.

Credit where it is due. And it makes a point worth making: these numbers can move. Which is precisely why the ones that aren't moving are a choice, not an act of God.

Where Reform UK Stands

Nobody in this country should be treated in a corridor, and nobody should wait eighteen months for an operation. The staff are not the problem — they are holding the thing together through a record summer. The problem is a system that swallows ever more money and delivers ever longer queues, run by people whose answer to every failure is another reorganisation and another target to miss.

Reform UK's position is that the money has to reach the front line rather than the layers above it, that management and consultancy bloat should be cut to fund staff and beds, and that a target missed by half as much again should cost someone their job rather than earn them a bonus. Above all, the honest starting point is this: judge the NHS by whether your mum gets a bed, not by whether a minister gets a soundbite.
