Healey's First Budget Lands on 28 October and Britain Is Already Bracing for the Bill

The date is set, the rhetoric is warm, and the arithmetic is brutal. Economists put the hole in the public finances at a minimum of 22 billion pounds. Every Labour Budget of this Parliament has closed a gap the same way.

On 31 July, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, John Healey, released a video message confirming that his first Budget will be delivered to the House of Commons on 28 October 2026. “This will be a budget that moves money and power out of Westminster, and into every postcode around Britain,” he said. It is a good line. It is also, at this stage, only a line.

The Number Behind the Slogan

Strip away the language about postcodes and the position is straightforward. Economists have warned that Healey faces a fiscal gap of at least 22 billion pounds as he tries to reconcile the Government's spending commitments with its own self-imposed borrowing rules. That is the sum that has to be found somewhere between now and late October.

There are three ways to close a gap of that size. You can cut spending, which this Government has shown no appetite for. You can borrow more, which would mean tearing up the fiscal rules ministers keep insisting are inviolable. Or you can raise taxes. On the record of the last two years, only one of those three is a serious prospect.

This is Healey's first Budget, delivered for a Prime Minister, Andy Burnham, who himself arrived in Downing Street without facing the country. Neither man has a personal mandate for a tax-raising Budget. Both are about to deliver one.

Speculation Is Not the Same as a Plan

Changes to capital gains tax, to property taxation and to other wealth-related taxes have all been discussed widely in the press. Almost none of it has been formally confirmed. I want to be careful here, because guessing at Budget measures and reporting them as fact is exactly the sort of thing that erodes trust in political commentary.

What can be said with confidence is this: the gap is real, the date is fixed, and the Government has ruled out the alternatives to taxation. The rest is arithmetic that anyone can do at their own kitchen table.

What also deserves saying is that uncertainty has a cost of its own. Businesses deciding whether to hire, families deciding whether to move, savers deciding what to do with a pension pot — all of them now have eleven weeks of speculation to sit through before they learn the rules. That paralysis is not free.

Devolution Rhetoric, Westminster Reality

The promise to move money and power “into every postcode” deserves scrutiny rather than applause. As a councillor, I have watched a decade of governments of both colours announce the decentralisation of power while decentralising something rather different: responsibility for cuts.

Real devolution means local authorities with genuine control over the money they raise and how they spend it. What is usually delivered is a new structure, a new mayor, a new set of bidding rounds, and the same Whitehall officials holding the purse strings. If 28 October brings the former, I will say so. I am not counting on it.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK's answer to a 22 billion pound gap does not start with the taxpayer. It starts with the state. That means unwinding the thresholds freeze that has quietly dragged millions of working people into higher tax bands without a single rate ever being raised in the Commons. It means taking a serious blade to the cost of the asylum accommodation system, to consultancy spending, and to the quango layer that has grown steadily through every fiscal crisis of the last fifteen years.

Above all it means an honest Budget. Tell people what you are taking and why, rather than raising billions through frozen allowances and calling it something else. Britain does not have a revenue problem so much as a spending discipline problem, and no amount of talk about postcodes changes that.
