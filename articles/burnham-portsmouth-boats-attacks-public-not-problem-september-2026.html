The Boats Reached Hampshire — and Burnham Turned His Fire on the Public, Not the Problem

A boat launched 170 miles away reached Hampshire's shores for the first time, and hundreds of ordinary residents turned out in anger. Andy Burnham's government's response was not to fix the failure — it was to brand the public 'thuggish' and accuse them of 'crossing the line'. When the state treats your reaction as the problem, that has a name: deflection.

Something happened on the south coast this weekend that ought to alarm anyone who believes a government's first job is to control its own borders. A small boat reached Hampshire — and the people in power decided the real villains were the people of Portsmouth.

What Actually Happened

On Sunday, a boat that had launched from Baie de Seine — around 170 miles from Calais — landed more than 100 men on the shore near Portsmouth, reaching that stretch of Hampshire coast for the first time. Hundreds of local residents turned out in protest. There was a standoff with police as people tried to stop the arrivals being bused to an asylum processing facility.

So how did the Government respond to a boat reaching a brand-new stretch of English coastline? It condemned the residents. Ministers branded the crowd "intimidating and thuggish" and said they had "crossed the line." Reform UK's response was blunt and, frankly, fair: it accused Andy Burnham of "directly inciting disorder" by making the people the enemy.

The Oldest Trick in the Book

Let me be honest about what this is. When a government cannot solve a problem, it reframes the anger about the problem as the problem itself. The boats keep coming, so instead of talking about the boats, they talk about you — your tone, your anger, your "thuggish" reaction. It is deflection dressed up as moral concern. Some would call it gaslighting: being told that your entirely rational alarm at losing control of the border is the thing that ought to frighten you.

And notice the sleight of hand. Ministers keep repeating that crossings are "down more than 40%." Perhaps — but a boat still launched from 170 miles away and made it to Hampshire for the first time, despite a fresh deal for more officers on French beaches. A statistic on a press release is no comfort to a coastal town watching dinghies arrive on a beach that never used to see them.

Be Clear About the People

Let's be fair, because the argument is stronger for it. Nobody should excuse genuine violence or intimidation, and protest must always stay within the law. But the overwhelming majority who turned out in Portsmouth were not extremists. They were ordinary residents — worried, angry, and utterly reasonable in wanting their country to control who arrives on its shores. To smear an entire town as a mob because a government is embarrassed by its own failure is a slur on decent people.

I stand with the British people who want their borders defended. That is not extremism. It is the most basic expectation a citizen can have of their state.

Where Reform UK Stands

The answer to boats on your beaches is to stop the boats — not to smear the people who are alarmed that they are there. Reform UK would take control of the Channel and return arrivals to the safe country they left, so the crossing becomes pointless and the dinghies stop. And we would never treat the legitimate concern of the British public as a public-order problem to be lectured away. Andy Burnham had a choice this weekend: confront the failure, or attack the people upset by it. He chose to attack the people. That tells you everything about who this Government is really protecting — and it isn't you.
