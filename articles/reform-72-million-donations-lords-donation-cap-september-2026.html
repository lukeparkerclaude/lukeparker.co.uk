£72 Million in 48 Hours — and Suddenly Westminster Cares About Big Money

Ben Delo gave Reform UK £36 million. Within 24 hours, Christopher Harborne matched him. £72 million in two days — reported as the biggest political donations this country has ever seen, and together worth roughly three-quarters of what every party spent, combined, at the 2024 general election. And within a day of the second cheque landing, Westminster rediscovered a burning passion for capping donations. Let's be honest about what that sequence tells you.

Here is the timeline, and I would ask you to hold it in your head while the commentary washes over you this week.

Ben Delo, the co-founder of the crypto exchange BitMEX, gave Reform UK £36 million. Within 24 hours, Christopher Harborne, a Tether investor and already the party's largest donor, matched it pound for pound. Two men, two days, £72 million.

Within another day, the calls for a cap on political donations were louder than they have been in a decade.

What Actually Happened

The scale is not in dispute. The two gifts are reported as the largest donations in British political history, and together they are worth roughly three-quarters of the total spent by all parties combined at the 2024 general election. That is not a rounding error in the funding of British politics. It is a step change, and anybody who pretends otherwise is not being straight with you.

Harborne described his gift as "a philanthropic gift to our country", intended to help the party prepare for government. Reform has said neither man will receive a knighthood, a peerage or a government contract if the party enters office. That commitment matters, and it should be held to — by supporters first, not just by opponents.

Now Watch the Timing

Parliament has been chewing over party funding all year. Last week MPs backed a £100,000 cap on donations from overseas voters and a suspension of funding through cryptoassets. Both still need to clear the House of Lords, where the Representation of the People Bill begins its stages on Monday.

What was conspicuously absent from all of that? A cap on donations from people living in Britain. The Labour backbencher Stella Creasy has been trying to fix that for months. She has tabled a revised amendment for an immediate £500,000 cap on donations to UK parties, falling to £100,000 the following year, with a permanent cap set afterwards on Electoral Commission advice. A separate Creasy amendment for a flat £100,000 cap straight away has picked up 46 signatures.

Andy Burnham's government says it is determined to tackle "mega-donors", and a minister has said a team is working at pace on a solution. Byline Times reported at the start of this month that he faces a backlash over an apparent retreat from an earlier cap pledge.

So: a cap was a nice idea in the spring, went quiet over the summer, and became a five-alarm emergency the moment the money started flowing to the wrong party. My honest read is that this is not a principle. It is a reflex.

Let's Be Honest About the Awkward Bit

I am not going to insult you by pretending there is no question here.

A party that takes £72 million from two individuals has its funding concentrated in very few hands, and voters are entitled to ask what those hands expect. That is a fair question. It is the same fair question that should have been asked of every party bankrolled by a handful of hedge funds, a handful of unions, or a handful of peers-in-waiting, for as long as any of us can remember. The answer cannot be "it is fine when it is us".

There is a serious, grown-up case for a donation cap that applies to everyone: individuals, companies, and trade union political funds alike. There is no case at all for a cap designed, drafted and timed to disadvantage one party while leaving the incumbents' own plumbing untouched.

Where Reform UK Stands

Write the rules for the next hundred years, not for the next opinion poll. Foreign money out. Full, fast transparency on every pound, from every source. And if Parliament wants a cap on domestic donations, then cap them — properly, symmetrically, and with union political funds and corporate giving inside the same fence as private individuals.

What we should not accept is a Parliament that shrugged at concentrated party funding for decades and discovered its conscience in the 48 hours after Reform UK banked a large cheque. The establishment is not frightened of big money in politics. It is frightened of big money it does not control. Pass a fair cap and we will live with it. Rig one, and everybody will see exactly what you did.
