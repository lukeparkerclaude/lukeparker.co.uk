Asylum Hotels Halved — But 16,000 Are Still in Them and the Bill Is £4.36 Billion

The number of asylum seekers in hotels has halved, and it would be churlish not to say so. But 16,021 are still in them, 93,293 people are still supported, and the Home Office spent £4.36 billion last year. Let's be honest about what that means.

Let me give credit first, because accuracy matters more to me than point-scoring. The latest Home Office figures show 16,021 asylum seekers in hotels at the end of June 2026, down 50% from 32,041 a year earlier, the lowest since the data was first reported in 2022. Fewer than 160 hotels are in use now, and 13 closed in the month before the figures were published. That is a real change, and I welcome it.

But Look at What Is Left

Sixteen thousand people are still in hotels. And the hotels are not the whole story. Dispersal accommodation, the houses and flats in our communities, rose 4% to 69,038. In total, 93,293 people are still receiving asylum support. The numbers have shifted from hotel rooms to other places; they have not vanished.

The Bill

Home Office asylum spending was £4.36 billion in 2025-26, down 8% from £4.76 billion the year before. A fall, yes, and I will not pretend it is not. But £4.36 billion is still an enormous sum, and the Home Office itself notes that the figure excludes the cost of intercepting small boats. Think of what that money could do in Lancashire: potholes, social care, council services we are constantly told we cannot afford.

Why the Numbers Fell

The Oxford Migration Observatory's Dr Nuni Jorgenson says hotels have mostly closed because decisions are now being made faster. The backlog awaiting an initial decision fell 56% to 40,168. That is competence, and it is the type of competence that should have been there years ago. My honest read: it is the product of pressure from voters, not goodwill from the system.

Where Reform UK Stands

Closing hotels is not the same as ending the problem. We want illegal arrivals deterred and removed, not simply moved from one kind of taxpayer-funded accommodation to another. Claims must be decided quickly, those with no right to be here must leave, and the British taxpayer should not be funding a £4 billion-plus system that exists because the front door was left open.

Source: Press Association / Express & Star, “Number of asylum seekers living in hotels halved” (27 August 2026), reporting Home Office data: 16,021 in hotels at end June 2026 vs 32,041; dispersal accommodation 69,038 (+4%); 93,293 receiving support; £4.36bn asylum spending 2025-26 vs £4.76bn; 40,168 awaiting initial decision (-56%); Migration Observatory comment.
