Same Words, Different County: Britain's Speech-Arrest Postcode Lottery

Everyone has seen the headline figure: over 62,000 arrests for online communications offences in five years. The detail underneath it is what should worry you more. Cumbria Police arrested 25.7 people per 10,000 — roughly two and a half times the national average. Northumbria, right next door, arrested 1.9. Same law. Same words. Thirteen times the chance of a knock at the door, depending on which side of a county line you live on.

The number that made the headlines was 62,000. Over five years, more than 62,000 people arrested in the UK for online communications offences, producing 18,510 charges and 12,292 convictions, according to research published this month by Big Brother Watch.

That figure has been argued about for a fortnight. I want to talk about the bit underneath it, because I think it matters more and almost nobody has picked it up.

The Postcode Lottery

Cumbria Police arrested 25.7 people per 10,000 over five years — around two and a half times the national average.

Neighbouring Northumbria arrested 1.9 per 10,000.

Read that again. Same statute book. Same words typed into the same apps. Roughly thirteen times the chance of being arrested for it, determined by which side of a county boundary your broadband happens to land on.

Big Brother Watch attributes this to what it calls the elasticity of vaguely defined speech offences in legislation including the Online Safety Act — laws loose enough that two neighbouring forces can read them completely differently and both be within the rules.

And this is not remote for us. Cumbria is our neighbour. The people being arrested at two and a half times the national rate are a short drive up the M6.

Let's Be Honest About the Caveats

I am not going to run this figure without the qualifications, because the qualifications matter and leaving them out would make me exactly the sort of person I complain about.

Arrest is not conviction. Of those 62,000-odd arrests, around 18,500 led to a charge and about 12,300 to a conviction — so the majority of arrests did not end in a court finding anything. Some of those arrests will have been for genuinely serious things: threats to kill, stalking, targeting children. Nobody sensible wants those left alone, and I don't.

Force-level rates can also be affected by how each force records data, and a single large operation can move a smaller force's numbers a long way. Cumbria is not a big force.

But none of that explains a thirteen-fold gap. Recording differences shift figures at the margin. They do not turn 1.9 into 25.7.

Why This Is the Real Scandal

My honest read is this. A law that is applied this differently is not really one law. It is thirty-odd local interpretations of a phrase, and the citizen has no way of knowing in advance which one applies to them.

That is the opposite of how law is supposed to work. You are meant to be able to read a rule and know whether you are on the right side of it. When the answer depends on your force area rather than your conduct, the rule has stopped being a rule and become a lottery.

And the chilling effect does not need a conviction to work. Being arrested is enough — the door, the phone seized, the interview, the months of waiting, the employer who now knows. Roughly 50,000 of those arrests produced no conviction at all. That is not nothing that happened to those people. That is the process being the punishment.

Two-thirds of MPs who commented on the legislation supported changing the Act or replacing it altogether. There is a majority for reform. There just isn't a government interested in finding it.

Where Reform UK Stands

Free speech is not free if it depends on your postcode. A law that can be read as 1.9 arrests per 10,000 in one county and 25.7 in the next is too vague to be on the statute book in that form, whatever anyone intended when they wrote it.

Big Brother Watch is calling for an independent national review of the laws affecting free speech in this country. That is a modest, reasonable ask and the government should grant it tomorrow. Police time spent on offensive posts is police time not spent on the 82% of offences that currently result in nobody being charged.

Britain has never needed a law to tell people not to be unpleasant online. It has needed, and still needs, a law clear enough that a citizen can read it and know where the line is. We do not have one. The figures from Cumbria and Northumbria prove it.
