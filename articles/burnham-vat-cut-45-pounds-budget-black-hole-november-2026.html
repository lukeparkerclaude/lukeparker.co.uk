£45 a Year Off Your Electricity — and a £22bn Hole Behind It. Who Pays at the Budget?

The Prime Minister's headline cost-of-living move is worth about £45 a year to the average household. Let's be honest about the bill that comes with it — and who ends up paying.

It was sold as help. VAT taken off electricity bills from 1 October, saving the average household about £45 a year. Andy Burnham told ministers: "We need to be a cost-of-living government."

Fine. Every pound back in a family's pocket is welcome. But a number like £45 deserves to be held up against everything around it.

The Cost, and the Funding

According to the wire report, the VAT cut was priced at about £850 million in the current financial year. The government said it would be funded by scrapping Starmer's digital ID scheme, which it said would have cost taxpayers £1.8 billion.

Not everyone bought it. Former minister Darren Jones argued the ID programme was "unfunded" and wrote that "the government will have to set out how it will pay for its new policies at the budget." City AM reported in July that the funding source had not been identified, and put the estimated annual cost of removing VAT from energy bills at £1.7 billion.

The £22bn Question

City AM's analysis, published 22 July, found about £22 billion of spending pledges not accounted for in the official forecasts — a total that also takes in higher debt interest, which it linked to the Iran war, and higher-than-expected borrowing. The paper listed further mooted proposals — unfreezing the personal allowance, nationalising Thames Water, and raising foreign aid to 0.7% of GDP — with a combined cost of at least £45 billion.

The independent economist Julian Jessop said tax rises are "pretty much guaranteed". Helen Thomas of Blonde Money put it more sharply: "Rachel Reeves said she inherited a black hole, now she passes one on."

You Have Heard This Before

City AM noted that Rachel Reeves raised £66 billion in tax through her two main fiscal interventions. Families were told that was the price of fixing the books. Now a new Chancellor, John Healey, says "fiscal control is the first duty of any Chancellor" — and the pledges keep arriving.

My honest read: a £45 saving on one bill is a gesture. If it is followed by tax rises at the Budget, working families will have paid for it several times over. I cannot tell you what the Budget will contain — nobody outside the Treasury can — but the warnings from economists are on the record.

Where Reform UK Stands

The way to lower energy bills is to lower the cost of energy, not to shave the VAT and send the bill somewhere else. Government should stop spending money it hasn't got, find the savings in Whitehall waste before it reaches into your wages, and be straight with the public about what it is paying for. That is the standard we hold ourselves to on Lancashire County Council, and it is the standard Westminster should meet.

Source: VAT on electricity from 1 October, ~£45 average saving, ~£850m cost this financial year, £1.8bn digital ID funding, Burnham and Darren Jones quotes: Courthouse News (wire report, undated). £22bn, £1.7bn, £45bn, £66bn, Jessop, Thomas and Healey quotes: City AM, 22 July 2026. Figures are as reported by those outlets; the Budget contents are not known.
