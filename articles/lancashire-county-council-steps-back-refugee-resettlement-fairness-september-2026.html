Why Lancashire Is Stepping Back From Running Refugee Resettlement

On Thursday, Lancashire County Council's cabinet voted to end the authority's role coordinating five Home Office-funded refugee resettlement schemes by 31 March 2027 at the latest. The opposition has called it in and thrown around the word 'xenophobia'. The real argument is about fairness, and about who should be running housing-linked support in the first place.

On Thursday, Lancashire County Council's cabinet voted unanimously to end the authority's role as coordinating body for five Home Office-funded refugee resettlement schemes, with a managed handover to be completed by 31 March 2027 at the latest. Four of the schemes cover Afghans who assisted British forces; the fifth is the UK Resettlement Scheme for other nationalities. Around 745 people are currently supported, out of about 1,250 helped since the schemes began. All of them arrived legally and have the right to remain, and nothing in this decision changes that.

What the Decision Does, and Doesn't, Do

Let's be precise, because the opposition hasn't been. The council is not "throwing anyone out". Refugees already living in Lancashire keep every statutory service they're entitled to. The council's own report says withdrawing "would not automatically result in an end to refugees resettling in Lancashire". What changes is who administers the schemes: most likely the district councils, which, unlike County Hall, already hold housing responsibilities. The council has claimed £18m from government since 2020 to run these programmes, and that funding follows the schemes, not the county.

The Fairness Argument

Cabinet member Joshua Roberts set out the case, and it deserves to be heard rather than shouted down. The schemes provide a named caseworker for up to five years, help with GP registration, school places, benefits, and work, plus regular check-ins. That is a serious, well-funded package. As Cllr Roberts put it, there is no equivalent coordinated offer for any other vulnerable group in Lancashire, while other residents have to go through five or six different doors to get the same help. My colleague Matthew Salter pointed to the 42,000 people on Lancashire's housing waiting list and called that the heart of the issue.

Being fair to the critics: the report notes most of the 350 properties used under these schemes were private rentals rather than social housing, and that under the main Afghan programme most families find their own accommodation. So this isn't a magic fix for the waiting list, and nobody should pretend it is. My honest read is that the principle still stands. A council should not run a gold-standard support service for one group that it cannot offer to the pensioner, the care leaver, or the veteran down the road.

The Row and the Call-In

The response from Progressive Lancashire was to accuse Reform of using refugees as "guinea pigs" and to brand Facebook posts on the subject "xenophobic". The Lib Dem leader called us "morally bankrupt and financially dumb". All the opposition groups have now called the decision in, so it will go to a cross-party scrutiny committee. Fine. Scrutiny is what committees are for, and the case will be made again there. But note what the critics are doing: they'd rather call names than answer the fairness question.

Where Reform UK Stands

Reform UK was elected in Lancashire last year on a clear promise to do things differently, and this decision was flagged before polling day, not sprung afterwards. We believe local services should be fair to everyone who lives here, that housing-linked support belongs with the councils that hold housing powers, and that Britain's obligations to those who served alongside our forces are a national responsibility, properly funded and honestly administered, not quietly parked on County Hall. This is what change looks like. It will be scrutinised, and it should be. We'll make the argument every time.
