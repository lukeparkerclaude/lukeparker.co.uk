Lancashire's Council Shake-Up Is On Hold Because Whitehall Isn't Sure It Was Legal

On Monday 7 September the Communities Secretary stood up in the Commons and paused local government reorganisation across most of England — withdrawing decisions already taken in four areas and halting 14 others — because, in light of legal advice, she wants to be sure the process complies with the law. Lancashire submitted five competing proposals last November. Shadow elections were pencilled in for May 2027. Nobody in County Hall can now tell you what happens next.

On Monday afternoon, 7 September, the Communities Secretary Angela Rayner stood up in the House of Commons and pulled the handbrake on local government reorganisation across most of England.

Not paused for a fortnight while a form is signed. A full review. Decisions already taken in four areas — Essex, Hampshire, Norfolk and Suffolk and their surrounding areas — withdrawn. Work in 14 other areas halted. And the stated reason was legal advice: she wanted to satisfy herself that the process in place is robust and, in her words, "complies with the law".

What This Means for Lancashire

Lancashire is not a bystander here. Councils across the county developed their preferred options and submitted five separate proposals to government in November 2025 — five, because nobody could agree. Elections to new shadow councils had been confirmed for May 2027. The new unitary councils were set to launch in April 2028, at which point the existing councils would cease to exist.

All of that now sits in a queue behind a legal review with no published end date. Every officer hour, every consultant's report, every committee evening spent arguing about boundaries over the past year is now, at best, on ice.

And notice what has not changed. Rayner told the Commons the government's ambition to have new strategic authorities in place across England by the end of 2028 remains the same. So the destination is unchanged, the route is suspended, and the deadline is untouched. Anyone who has ever run a project will recognise that combination, and will know how it ends: the time gets taken out of the consultation, not out of the ambition.

Getting the Basics Wrong

Let's be honest about what actually happened. This is a government that spent over a year telling councils to tear up their structures, abolish themselves, merge with neighbours and reorganise hundreds of thousands of residents' services — and has now stopped the whole thing because it is not confident the process it designed stands up legally.

That is not a technicality. When a Secretary of State withdraws decisions she has already made, in four separate areas, that is Whitehall conceding it moved faster than its own lawyers. The people who will pay for that are not in Whitehall. They are the council staff who have spent a year unsure whether their job exists in 2028, and the residents who were told this was all urgent and necessary and settled.

My honest read is that reorganisation was always being driven by the wrong thing. It was never really about better bins, better roads or better care. It was about drawing maps that make mayors possible — and Lancashire having an elected mayor was widely understood to be the natural endpoint of all this. Structures first, services second. That is precisely the wrong way round, and it is why five different Lancashire proposals could exist at once: when the exercise is about power rather than outcomes, everybody draws the map that suits them.

What Should Happen Now

The pause is an opportunity, provided nobody wastes it. Use it to publish, in plain numbers, what reorganisation has already cost Lancashire in officer time and external advice. Use it to ask residents directly whether they want a mayor, rather than arriving at one by way of a boundary review most people have never heard of. And use it to fix the thing the public actually notices — potholes, SEND provision, adult social care, planning decisions that take months — because none of those improve because a logo changed on a letterhead.

Where Reform UK Stands

Reform UK runs Lancashire County Council, and our position is that structures should follow services, not the other way round. We were elected to fix what people actually experience: the state of the roads, the cost of the council, the quality of children's services, the value delivered for every pound of council tax. That job does not change because Whitehall has stopped its own reorganisation to check its homework.

What I would say to Lancashire residents is this. Do not be anxious about the pause. Be sceptical of what comes after it. When the review reports, the pressure will be to make up lost time by cutting short the bit where the public gets a say — and if devolution is going to mean anything at all, that is the one part that cannot be rushed.

A government that cannot be sure its own reorganisation process is lawful has not earned the right to tell Lancashire how Lancashire should be governed. Let them do their review. We will carry on doing the job.
