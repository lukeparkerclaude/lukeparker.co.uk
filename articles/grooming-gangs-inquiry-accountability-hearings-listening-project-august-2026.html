The Grooming Gangs Inquiry Finally Reaches The Accountability Stage — And Not A Moment Too Soon

This month the statutory inquiry into grooming gangs opened its National Listening Project and began taking core participant applications for its first national accountability hearings. Institutions will be compelled to explain themselves in public. It has taken far too long to get here.

This month the Statutory Independent Inquiry into Grooming Gangs launched its National Listening Project and opened core participant applications for its National Accountability Hearing. It is a procedural announcement, written in the flat language such things are always written in. It is also the most significant step this inquiry has taken.

Because core participant status is what gives survivors standing. And accountability hearings are where institutions stop issuing statements and start answering questions under compulsion, in public, on the record.

Where The Inquiry Has Got To

The inquiry formally began its work on 31 March 2026. It is chaired by Baroness Anne Longfield CBE, the former Children’s Commissioner for England, sitting with panellists Zoë Billingham CBE and Eleanor Kelly CBE. It is time-limited to three years and has a budget of £65 million.

Its local investigations examine what happened in specific places, beginning with Oldham, Bradford and Keighley, and London. In each, the inquiry is looking at how the gangs operated, what institutions knew, how they responded, and why children were not protected.

The inquiry has said it intends to hold its first national public accountability hearings by the end of this year, with individuals and institutions compelled to explain publicly what they did and did not do. Compelled is the operative word. For two decades the pattern in these towns was that nobody was ever obliged to account for anything, and the record shows precisely what that produced.

Why The Delay Still Matters

It is worth being clear about the timeline. Rotherham was documented in detail in 2014. Rochdale, Telford, Oldham and others followed. It took until 2025 for a national inquiry to be announced, and until 31 March 2026 for it to begin work — and that announcement came only after sustained public and political pressure that the government initially resisted.

Survivors who were children when this happened to them are now in their thirties and forties. Some have spent longer waiting for an inquiry than they spent being abused. Officials who made decisions in 2005 have retired on full pensions. Records have been lost. Memories have faded in ways that will make accountability harder to establish than it would have been a decade ago.

The three-year limit is therefore a double-edged commitment. It guards against an inquiry that drifts for a decade and reports into a country that has stopped listening. But three years is not long to cover multiple towns, decades of institutional failure, and the volume of evidence involved. The inquiry will have to be disciplined about scope, and it will face pressure from every direction to widen it.

The Question That Has To Be Answered

The central question is not whether the abuse happened. That is established beyond argument. It is why, over so many years and in so many places, institutions that existed to protect children instead protected themselves.

Part of the answer is resourcing and part is competence. But a substantial part is that officials were reluctant to pursue lines of inquiry they feared would be characterised as racist, and so children — overwhelmingly white working-class girls, in towns nobody in Westminster had visited — were treated as making lifestyle choices rather than as victims of organised rape.

That has been said out loud in previous reviews and it needs saying again in this one, without euphemism, because an inquiry that flinches from it will have wasted £65 million and three years. Equally, an inquiry that treats it as the whole explanation will let a great many negligent individuals off the hook. Both things need to be established, in public, with names attached.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK argued for a full statutory national inquiry with powers of compulsion long before the government conceded one, and that position has been vindicated. What matters now is what happens with it.

The inquiry must be given whatever it needs to compel evidence and witnesses, including from police forces and local authorities that have historically been reluctant to disclose. Where the evidence supports it, individuals should face professional and criminal consequences rather than the quiet retirements that have so far been the norm. And recommendations should carry a statutory duty to implement, because the shelves of British public life are heavy with reports that changed nothing.

Survivors have been failed by councils, by police forces, by social services and by successive governments. The accountability hearings are the first real opportunity for that to be put on the record and answered for. Whatever else is disputed in British politics, this should not be: those hearings must be public, they must be thorough, and they must name names.
