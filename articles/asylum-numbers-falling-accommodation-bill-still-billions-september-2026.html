Crossings Are Down. The Bill Is Still Enormous. That Tells You Everything.

Let's start with the honest bit: the numbers are falling. Around 16,400 people crossed the Channel between January and 1 September, roughly 43% fewer than the same period last year. Asylum claims are down 21%. The backlog is down 56%. Good. Now here is the question nobody in Whitehall wants asked — if the volume is falling this fast, why is the bill still measured in billions?

I want to do something unusual in politics and start by conceding a point.

The small boats numbers have come down. Between 1 January and 1 September 2026, somewhere around 16,400 people crossed the Channel in small boats — around 43% fewer than in the same period of 2025, with summer crossings at their lowest level since 2019. Asylum applications fell too: 85,891 in the twelve months to the end of June, down 21% on the year before and the lowest annual total since June 2022. The number of people waiting for an initial decision dropped 56% over the year to June, to 40,168 — the lowest since March 2019.

If a number has fallen, I am going to say it has fallen. That is how you keep your credibility. So: those are real improvements, and I will not pretend otherwise.

Now the Question Nobody Wants

Here it is. If the arrivals are down 43%, the claims are down 21% and the backlog is down 56% — why is the money still going the wrong way?

The National Audit Office examined the Home Office's asylum accommodation contracts and found that arrangements originally estimated at a total cost of £4.5 billion are now expected to cost around £15.3 billion over ten years. That is not a rounding error. That is more than three times the original estimate.

And the detail is worse than the headline. In 2024/25, hotel accommodation accounted for 76% of the annual cost of those asylum contracts — while housing only 35% of the people in asylum accommodation. Three-quarters of the money. A third of the people.

Meanwhile the Home Office's own figures put the cost of dealing with the 93,525 asylum applications lodged in the year to March 2026 at £1.7 billion. Officials have projected asylum costs of around £3.9 billion in 2026, £2.9 billion in 2027-28 and £2.5 billion in 2028-29. The Government's commitment is to end the use of hotels by 2029.

What That Actually Means

Falling numbers plus a rising bill can only mean one thing: the cost is not being driven by the volume. It is being driven by the system.

You are not paying through the nose because too many people arrived this summer. Fewer arrived this summer. You are paying because the state signed contracts it could not control, locked itself into the most expensive possible form of accommodation, and — as the NAO politely noted — left itself with limited tools to manage the expense. When hotels are more financially attractive to the supplier than the alternatives, guess what the supplier supplies.

This is the thing that never makes the news bulletin. Britain's asylum problem is not only an immigration problem. It is a procurement problem. It is a Whitehall that negotiates like an amateur and then sends the invoice to a family in Burnley who has just watched their energy bill go up £60 a year.

The Test That Matters

Ministers will point at the crossings graph and take a bow. Fine. But the public's test was never "is the line on the chart slightly lower than last August?" The public's test is simpler and harder: are we in control, and are we getting value for money?

A system that costs three times what it was contracted to cost is not under control. A system where three-quarters of the spend goes on a third of the people is not value for money. And a target to stop using hotels in 2029 — a full three years away, on the fourth or fifth government of the decade — is not a plan. It is a hope with a date attached.

Where Reform UK Stands

Give credit where the numbers fall — and then follow the money, because that is where the failure is hiding. Reform UK's position is straightforward: control the border properly, process claims quickly, remove those with no right to be here, and stop treating the taxpayer as an unlimited overdraft for contracts nobody in government appears to be managing.

Publish the contracts. Renegotiate or terminate the ones that are three times over their original estimate. End hotel use on a timetable measured in months, not in parliaments. And be honest with the country about the difference between arrivals falling and a system working — because right now, one is happening and the other plainly is not.
