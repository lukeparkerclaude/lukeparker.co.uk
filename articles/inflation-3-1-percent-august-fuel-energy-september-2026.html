Inflation Is Back Above 3% — and It's Energy and Fuel Doing It

Inflation jumped to 3.1% in the twelve months to August — the first reading above 3% since March. Motor fuel costs are up 23% on the year and household electricity and gas up 6%, while food inflation has eased to 1.3%. The Bank of England decides tomorrow. The Budget lands on 28 October. Let's be honest about why the bills keep climbing.

The figure landed at seven o'clock this morning, and it tells you more about the state of the country than any conference speech will this month.

Back Through Three Per Cent

Inflation jumped to 3.1% in the twelve months to August 2026. That is the first reading above 3% since March, and it comes on a morning when the Bank of England's rate-setters are already preparing to decide what to do tomorrow.

Look at what pushed it there, because the detail matters more than the headline. Motor fuel costs were up 23% on the year. The cost of electricity, gas and other household fuels rose 6% over the same twelve months. Food and non-alcoholic drink inflation, by contrast, eased to 1.3% in the twelve months to June, down from 1.7%.

So this is not a supermarket story. This is an energy story, and a fuel story, and therefore a getting-to-work story.

Let's Be Honest About What This Is

I am not going to pretend 3.1% is 2022 all over again. It isn't, and you would stop reading if I tried it.

Some of this is genuinely imported. Oil and gas prices have been pushed up by conflict in the Middle East, and no Chancellor of any party can vote that away. Food inflation coming down is real, and it is welcome, and I will say so. The Government's decision to take VAT off domestic electricity bills from 1 October is a real cut, and I would rather they did it than not.

But here is the bit that gets glossed over. A country that has spent fifteen years making itself more dependent on volatile imported gas has no defence when the price of volatile imported gas goes up. That is not bad luck. That is the predictable consequence of a policy choice made deliberately, and repeatedly, by both old parties.

And the VAT cut proves the point rather than answering it. From 1 October, Ofgem's cap still rises 4% to £1,723 a year for a typical dual-fuel household, up from £1,663 — after the VAT change is taken into account. You cannot subsidise your way out of expensive energy. You can only stop making energy expensive.

Two Dates Worth Writing Down

Thursday 17 September: the Bank of England decides. The Bank had expected CPI to reach 3.2% in the final three months of this year, and with oil and gas moving the way they are it may go higher than that. Anybody hoping for cheaper mortgages in the near term should temper their expectations.

Wednesday 28 October: the Chancellor, John Healey, delivers his first Budget, under a Prime Minister in Andy Burnham who has already hinted at tax rises and whom not one voter has been asked to approve.

My honest read is this. Prices are rising because energy and fuel are rising. Into that, a government is preparing to take more money out of household budgets. Whatever they call it on the day, that is a squeeze applied on top of a squeeze.

Where Reform UK Stands

Cheap energy is not an environmental question. It is the foundation of everything else. It is the price of the bus fare, the cost of the delivery van, the margin on the corner shop, and the heating in your front room. Get energy wrong and you get all of it wrong, which is precisely what has happened.

That means using what we have under our own feet and in our own waters rather than shipping it in at the world price and calling it progress. It means stripping out the levies and net-zero costs that have been quietly loaded onto bills for a decade, instead of trimming VAT at the end and hoping nobody does the sum. And it means being honest that pump prices are not a middle-class abstraction — in a county like ours, the car is not a luxury, it is how you get to work.

Tomorrow the Bank will tell you what it thinks. In six weeks the Chancellor will tell you what he wants. Neither will tell you the simple thing: Britain made its own energy expensive on purpose, and this is the bill.
