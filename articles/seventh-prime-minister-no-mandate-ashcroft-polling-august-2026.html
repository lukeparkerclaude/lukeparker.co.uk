Seventh Prime Minister in a Decade, and Not a Single Vote Cast For Him

Andy Burnham became Britain's seventh Prime Minister in a decade on 20 July. Lord Ashcroft's polling found only 27 per cent of Britons — and 45 per cent of Labour voters — thought he should take office without a leadership race. No general election is required until 2029.

Count them. Cameron, May, Johnson, Truss, Sunak, Starmer, Burnham. Seven Prime Ministers in ten years, and exactly two of them arrived at the door of Number Ten having won a general election as party leader. The rest were installed.

Andy Burnham took office on 20 July 2026. He is a serious politician with a real record in Greater Manchester and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But no member of the public voted to make him Prime Minister, and a very large number of them noticed.

The Coronation

The mechanics are worth spelling out, because they were unusual even by Westminster standards. In January 2026, Burnham tried to stand for Labour in the Gorton and Denton by-election and was blocked by his own party's National Executive Committee. Six months later a different route opened: Josh Simons resigned his seat in Makerfield, a by-election followed, and Burnham won it.

The leadership contest that was supposed to follow Sir Keir Starmer's departure did not really happen. An overwhelming majority of Labour's 403 MPs backed Burnham, and what had been billed as a race became, in the word used at the time, a coronation.

Lord Ashcroft's polling caught the public mood on this precisely. Only 27 per cent of Britons thought Burnham should become Prime Minister without a leadership race. Among Labour's own voters the figure was 45 per cent — still, notably, a minority. When fewer than half of your own side think the process was legitimate, the process was not legitimate.

Three Years Without Asking Anyone

Under the current rules, no general election is required until August 2029. That means a Prime Minister nobody voted for, leading a Cabinet nobody voted for, delivering a Budget on 28 October that was in nobody's manifesto, with three years of runway before the public gets any say at all.

And this is not a government coasting on continuity. Burnham has been explicit that he intends the largest changes in forty years — a new political model and a new economic model, in his own words from the steps of Downing Street. There are plans for essentials of life to be brought under stronger public control, a rolling programme of public ownership, and a substantial redistribution of power out of Westminster.

You may think some of that is a good idea. I think most of it is not. But that is precisely the point: it is a programme large enough that people should have been asked. A mandate is not a formality. It is the difference between governing a country and processing it.

The 2026 local elections gave some indication of what the public actually thinks of the outgoing arrangement. Labour lost control of 35 councils and close to 1,500 councillors — roughly 60 per cent of the seats it was defending. That is not a party the country was asking for more of.

The Bit I Am Obliged to Report Honestly

There is a fact here that does not flatter my own side, and readers of this site deserve it straight rather than buried.

Reform UK led the national polls for around eighteen months. We do not lead them today. Ipsos polling conducted between 30 July and 4 August 2026 put Labour three points ahead of Reform. Our average slipped from roughly 27 per cent to around 24 per cent following Nigel Farage's announcement on 7 July that he would resign his seat and re-contest it.

He then won Clacton back on 13 August with 63.3 per cent of the vote, which is an emphatic local result by any measure. But a strong by-election in a stronghold is not the same as a national lead, and I am not going to insult anyone's intelligence by pretending it is. Reform has ground to make up. The honest conclusion is that the case for a general election does not rest on our poll rating, and never did — it rests on the fact that a government with no mandate is now attempting the biggest changes in a generation.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK's position is straightforward and unchanged by our polling. A Prime Minister who takes office mid-parliament and then proposes a fundamental change of direction should seek a mandate for it. Not because the rules require it — they plainly do not — but because consent is what makes a government's decisions stick.

More broadly, we would look hard at a system that has produced seven Prime Ministers in a decade with almost no public involvement in five of those changes. That is not stability, whatever the people who benefit from it say. It is a governing class swapping the leadership among itself while the country watches.

So: call the election. Put the new economic model, the public ownership programme and the October Budget to the people who will live under them. If it is as popular as its authors believe, they have nothing to fear from asking.

My strong suspicion is that this is exactly why they will not.
