Britain Leaked Their Names and Then Told Them to Make Their Own Way Out

A single MoD email in February 2022 exposed details relating to more than 18,500 Afghan resettlement applications. It was not discovered for eighteen months. In April 2026 the Government told those still in Afghanistan to reach a third country under their own steam.

In February 2022 someone at the Ministry of Defence sent an email. Attached was a spreadsheet they believed held information on around 150 applicants to the Afghan Relocations and Assistance Policy. It did not. It held detailed personal information relating to more than 18,500 applications.

Nobody at the Ministry noticed. The breach was not discovered until August 2023, and only then because part of the dataset surfaced in a Facebook group. For eighteen months, a list of Afghans who had helped British forces was loose in the world and the department that leaked it had no idea.

Eighteen Months, and Then a Superinjunction

What followed was not a rapid public reckoning. It was a superinjunction — a court order so restrictive that its own existence could not be reported. The people whose names were on that spreadsheet could not read about what had happened to them in a British newspaper, because British newspapers were forbidden from telling them.

The order has since been lifted and the Commons Defence Committee has reported on the affair, under the pointed title Shifting heaven and earth?. Reporting in the year since the injunction fell has put the number of eligible Afghans still not resettled in the tens of thousands, and has attributed a number of deaths to the exposure. Those figures deserve scrutiny rather than repetition, and I would rather understate them than overstate them. But the shape of the thing is not in dispute: a serious failure, concealed for years, affecting people who took real risks on our behalf.

A further report published on 3 August 2026 found that Afghans who worked alongside UK forces had been left in danger by years of delay and flawed decision-making. That is four years after the email was sent.

The April Rule Change Nobody Shouted About

Then, in April 2026, the Ministry of Defence quietly changed the terms. It ended in-country assistance for movement out of Afghanistan. Eligible Afghans are now required to reach a safe third country independently, at their own expense and their own risk, before their cases can be processed — and to do it within twelve months.

Read that again. The state that leaked their details now requires them to smuggle themselves out of a country run by the people the leak may have exposed them to. Charities working with former interpreters have warned that for those who are destitute, in hiding, or too exposed to travel, a deadline of that kind functions less like a route and more like a door quietly closing.

The Sulha Alliance, which supports Afghan interpreters and other civilians who served with the British Army, has said it fears some who worked alongside British personnel will simply be left behind. Cases remain under review that have been under review for years.

This Is Not an Immigration Argument

I want to be careful here, because this subject gets flattened into a fight it is not part of. I believe in controlled immigration. I believe numbers matter, that a country is entitled to decide who settles in it, and that the last decade of policy has ridden roughshod over that principle. None of that is in tension with what I am about to say.

An interpreter who walked patrols with British soldiers is not an immigration statistic. He is a man who was given a promise by the British state in exchange for putting himself and his family in danger. A country that will not honour that promise will find the promise worth less the next time it needs someone to take the risk. That is not sentiment. It is the plain operational reality of how allies are recruited.

And there is a competence question underneath the moral one. A department that can email 18,500 records to the wrong place and not notice for a year and a half is not a department anyone should trust with a border, a procurement budget, or a war. The breach and the backlog are the same failure wearing different clothes.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK would settle the ARAP caseload rather than manage it indefinitely. That means a hard deadline for decisions on outstanding cases, published quarterly numbers so that progress can be checked by anyone who cares to look, and the restoration of practical assistance for those whose eligibility has already been accepted and who cannot move without help.

We would treat the data breach as the accountability failure it is. Somebody signed off the systems that allowed an 18,500-record file to leave the building attached to a routine email. Somebody decided that concealment was the right response. Parliament should know who, and what changed afterwards.

And we would separate this scheme, clearly and permanently, from the wider asylum system. A defined, time-limited obligation to people who served with our forces is not open-ended immigration, and pretending otherwise has allowed successive governments to hide one behind the other. Keep the promise, close the scheme, and be honest about both.

Four years, one leaked spreadsheet, thousands still waiting. Whatever else this government is short of, it is not short of time to have fixed it.
