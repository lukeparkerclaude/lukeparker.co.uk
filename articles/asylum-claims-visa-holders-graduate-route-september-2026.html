Four in Ten Asylum Claimants Already Had a Visa. That Is the Story Nobody Tells.

The Home Office figures for the year ending June 2026 tell two stories. The first is that asylum claims fell 21% to 85,891 and the initial decision backlog dropped 56% to its lowest level since 2019. Credit where it is due. The second is the one nobody puts in a press release: 41% of asylum claimants already held a visa or other leave to be here, and claims from former work visa holders rose 16%, driven largely by a more than doubling in claims from Graduate visa holders.

The Home Office published its immigration statistics for the year ending June 2026 on 27 August. I want to do something that is unfashionable in politics and start with the bit that does not suit my argument.

Yes, the Numbers Are Down. Say So.

Asylum claims fell. There were 85,891 claims in the year to June 2026, down 21% on the year before, and well below the peak of 109,662 in the year to September 2025. The backlog fell too, and sharply: 40,168 people were awaiting an initial decision at the end of June, relating to 32,401 cases — 56% lower than a year earlier and the lowest level since June 2019.

I am not going to stand here and tell you a fall is a rise. It is a fall. Anyone in my party who tells you otherwise is not worth listening to, because the moment you shade the numbers you have handed the argument away.

But a fall from a record is not the same as a system under control. Claims remain significantly above the levels typically seen before 2021. And the composition of who is claiming has quietly changed in a way that should bother anyone who thinks the border is about boats.

The Front Door Nobody Watches

Here is the figure that should be leading the news. Of the people claiming asylum in the year to June 2026, 41% — 34,999 people — previously held a visa or other leave to be in the United Kingdom. They did not arrive on a dinghy. They arrived at an airport, legally, with a document this country issued them.

Small boat arrivals accounted for 33,949 claimants, about 40% of the total. Read those two numbers next to each other. More asylum claimants came through the visa system than came across the Channel.

And within that, the trend is moving the wrong way. Claims from former study visa holders fell 35%. Claims from former work visa holders rose 16% — driven largely, in the Home Office's own words, by a more than doubling in claims from Graduate visa holders.

Let's be honest about what that pattern suggests. A route designed to let talented graduates stay and work for a couple of years is increasingly being used as a staging post into the asylum system when the leave runs out. That is not a failure of enforcement at the beach. It is a failure of design in Whitehall.

Four Times the Old Normal

The decision-making side tells its own story. The Home Office made 115,872 initial decisions, broadly unchanged on the year, but more than four times the annual average seen between 2010 and 2019. The grant rate fell to 38%, down from 48% the previous year and a long way from the 77% peak in the year to September 2022. Refusals rose 23% to 73,478.

Some of that shift is one specific collapse. The grant rate for Syrian claimants went from 98% to 10% after the fall of the Assad regime, which is exactly what a functioning system should do when conditions in a country change. Pakistan fell from 46% to 25%. Sudan sits at 92% and Eritrea at 84%; Bangladesh is at 11% and India at 1%.

That spread is the argument in a nutshell. When the grant rate for one nationality is 92% and for another it is 1%, the honest conclusion is that a very large number of people are making claims that were never going to succeed — and the state is spending four times the historic effort processing them.

Where Reform UK Stands

My honest read is that the government is getting better at the paperwork and no better at the policy. Processing faster is worth having. It is not the same as control.

Reform UK's position is that you cannot fix a border by looking only at the water. If four in ten claimants arrived on a visa we issued, then the visa system is part of the border and should be treated like it: proper scrutiny at the point of issue, routes like the Graduate visa reviewed against what actually happens when they expire, and an expectation that leave granted for a purpose ends when that purpose ends. Claims that are refused should result in removal, not in a decade of appeals. And ministers should publish the visa-route figures as prominently as the small boat figures, because the public is entitled to see the whole picture rather than the half of it that fits the day's announcement.

All figures — 85,891 claims, the 21% fall, the 109,662 peak in the year to September 2025, 34,999 claimants (41%) previously holding a visa or other leave, 33,949 small boat claimants, the 35% fall in claims from former study visa holders and 16% rise from former work visa holders including the more than doubling of Graduate visa claims, 115,872 initial decisions, the 38% grant rate, 73,478 refusals, the 40,168 people awaiting an initial decision, and the nationality grant rates — are as published in that release.
