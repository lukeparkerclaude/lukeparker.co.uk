135,580 Households In Temporary Accommodation: A Record No Government Should Be Proud Of

A record 135,580 households were living in temporary accommodation in England at the start of this year, among them 177,530 children. Councils have burned through more than £10 billion on it in five years, and the number keeps going up.

Between January and March this year, 135,580 households in England were living in temporary accommodation. It is the highest figure ever recorded. Among them were 177,530 children.

Not children who moved house. Children living in a hostel room, a bed and breakfast, a converted office block, or a flat in a town their parents have no connection to, with their belongings in bags and no idea how long it lasts. Households with children make up more than 63.8% of every household in temporary accommodation in this country.

Temporary Is The Wrong Word

The statutory term is doing an enormous amount of work here. “Temporary accommodation” suggests a few weeks between one home and the next. In practice families spend months and frequently years in it, moving between placements, changing schools, losing jobs because the commute from the new placement is impossible.

Parliament’s own Housing, Communities and Local Government Committee has examined conditions in temporary accommodation and found them wanting badly enough that the government was obliged to respond formally. A ministerial written statement in June acknowledged the need to tackle poor conditions. When a country needs a government response on the standard of accommodation it provides to homeless children, something has gone very wrong upstream.

The wider homelessness picture confirms it is not easing. The number of households owed a homelessness prevention duty in England rose by nearly 2% year on year in the first quarter of 2026. Relief duty cases fell by just over 5% — a modest improvement in one measure that does nothing to offset a record high in the other.

Ten Billion Pounds For Something Nobody Wants

Councils have spent more than £10 billion on temporary accommodation over the past five years. Ten billion pounds, and at the end of it not one family owns a home, not one council owns an additional housing unit, and the numbers are higher than when the spending started.

This is the most wasteful form of public spending imaginable. Money flows out of council budgets, into the hands of private landlords and hotel operators, and produces an asset for nobody. It is rent paid at emergency rates for accommodation that is often substandard, arranged at short notice because the alternative is leaving a family on the street.

The government points to over £3.6 billion committed from 2026/27 to 2028/29 for homelessness and rough sleeping services. That is real money and it will help people. But it is money for managing homelessness, and the reason homelessness keeps growing is that Britain has spent thirty years not building enough homes, and no amount of crisis funding substitutes for a house that does not exist.

The Pressure On Councils Is Now Structural

For many councils, temporary accommodation is now the single fastest-growing line in the budget, and it is a statutory duty — they cannot decline it. A council facing a homelessness surge has no choice but to fund it, which means cutting the discretionary services residents actually see: libraries, leisure centres, road maintenance, youth provision.

So residents experience two things simultaneously and are told they are unrelated. Their council tax rises. Their local services shrink. The connective tissue between those facts is often an emergency accommodation bill that has doubled in a few years because there is nowhere for people to live.

This is how a housing failure becomes a local government funding crisis becomes a decline in every visible public service in a town. It is all one problem.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK’s position is that you cannot fix a housing shortage while adding to demand faster than you add to supply. Bringing net migration down substantially is not a side issue to housing policy — it is central to it, because every year of high net migration adds household formation that the building rate has not come close to matching.

On supply, the priority is reforming a planning system that makes building slow, expensive and adversarial, and prioritising development on brownfield land so that homes go where infrastructure already exists. Councils should also be able to build again at scale rather than acting as procurement agents renting emergency rooms from the private sector at premium rates.

And there is a strong case for spending differently rather than simply more. Money currently going out of the door as emergency rent achieves nothing permanent. Directed instead at acquiring and building stock that councils own, it would reduce the bill every year thereafter rather than increasing it.

A record 135,580 households and 177,530 children is not a statistic to be noted in a quarterly release. It is a measure of how badly this country has failed at something it used to be able to do.
