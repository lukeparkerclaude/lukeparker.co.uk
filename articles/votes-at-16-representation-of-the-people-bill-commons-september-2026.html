They Just Changed Who Gets to Vote — and Nobody Asked You

On 2 September, MPs gave the Representation of the People Bill its third reading by 411 votes to 102 — a majority of 309. It lowers the voting age to 16 for Westminster and for English and Northern Irish local elections. Let's be honest about what happened here: the biggest change to who gets to vote in half a century, passed by a Parliament elected in 2024, under a Prime Minister who has never faced the country.

Changing the franchise is the single most serious thing a Parliament can do. It is the one decision that decides every other decision. And on Wednesday 2 September, the House of Commons did exactly that — and most people barely noticed.

What Actually Happened

MPs gave the Representation of the People Bill its third reading by 411 votes to 102 — a majority of 309. Labour supplied the largest bloc of Ayes, with 324 votes. The Conservatives supplied the largest bloc of Noes, with 95. The Bill now goes to the House of Lords.

What is in it? The headline measure is this: the voting age drops to 16 for UK Parliament elections and for local elections in England and Northern Ireland. Until now, 16 and 17-year-olds could only vote in certain Scottish and Welsh elections. The Bill also creates powers for more automated electoral registration, adds bank cards to the list of approved voter ID, and hands the Electoral Commission expanded enforcement powers.

Reform UK opposed lowering the voting age at second reading. So did the Conservatives and the TUV. Labour, the Liberal Democrats, the Greens and the SNP were in favour.

Let's Be Honest About the Good Bits

I am not going to pretend there is nothing worthwhile in this Bill, because there is, and you deserve straight talking rather than a pantomime.

Following the Rycroft Review into foreign financial interference — published on 25 March 2026, with 17 recommendations, all of which the Government has now accepted — ministers committed to amendments capping donations from overseas voters at £100,000 a year, imposing a moratorium on donations made in cryptoassets, adding donor location as a risk factor under "know your donor" rules, and basing company donation limits on post-tax profits rather than revenue. Committee stage also closed off a genuine loophole: unincorporated associations passing money to each other in chains to obscure where it came from.

Foreign money has no business in British elections. On that, credit where it is due.

The Part That Should Worry You

But here is my honest read. You cannot separate what was decided from who decided it.

This Parliament was elected in July 2024 on a manifesto and a leader the country has since discarded. Keir Starmer is gone — he announced on 1 September that he is quitting as an MP altogether. Andy Burnham sits in Downing Street having been installed by his party in July, not chosen by the public. Not one voter in this country has ever been asked whether they want him running the place.

And that administration's early legislative test — its first big win back from summer recess — was to change the rules of the game itself.

Sixteen-year-olds cannot buy a pint. They cannot buy a lottery ticket, get a mortgage, sit on a jury, stand for the council they would now be voting for, or in most cases leave education. We have spent a decade raising the age at which we treat people as adults for almost everything else. On this one thing, and only this one thing, the age comes down. You are allowed to ask why.

And the Bit They Didn't Do

Meanwhile, New Clause 31 — tabled by Labour backbencher Alex Sobel and supported by more than 170 MPs — would have required a report on setting up a National Commission on Electoral Reform to look at the voting system itself. Because of course the voting system is fine when it works for you.

Here is the thing about first past the post that establishment parties have understood for a century: it is not a principle, it is a machine. And they will only ever look at fixing it when it stops delivering for them.

Where Reform UK Stands

If you want to change who chooses the Government, you should first have the courage to face the people you already have. Reform UK opposed lowering the voting age, and we were right to. Eighteen is the age of majority in this country — make it mean something, consistently, or explain honestly why it only bends in one direction.

Clean up foreign money, absolutely. Tighten donation rules, yes. But an unelected Prime Minister, propped up by a Parliament elected on a prospectus the country has already rejected, rewriting the franchise in his second month in office is not democratic renewal. It is the establishment adjusting the furniture while the house burns. Call a general election. Let the country speak. Then change the rules with a mandate — not without one.
