A Pen Stroke at the OBR, a £20 Billion Hole, and a Bill Arriving on 28 October

Chancellor John Healey delivers his first Budget on 28 October. A modest downgrade to the OBR's productivity assumption could open a gap of around £20 billion by the end of the decade. Labour has ruled out the three taxes everyone can see. That leaves the ones you cannot.

There is a number sitting in a spreadsheet at the Office for Budget Responsibility that will do more to shape your household finances this autumn than any speech from any minister. It is the assumed rate of productivity growth. Nobody votes on it. Almost nobody outside the Treasury could tell you what it currently is. And if it moves by three tenths of one per cent, the consequences land on your payslip.

Chancellor John Healey confirmed on 31 July that his first Budget will be delivered on Wednesday 28 October, and commissioned the OBR to produce a full economic and fiscal forecast to sit alongside it. That forecast is where the trouble lives.

How a Rounding Error Becomes £20 Billion

The mechanism is not complicated, though it is designed to sound as though it is. The OBR forecasts how fast the economy can grow without generating inflation. Faster growth means more tax revenue arriving automatically over the years ahead. The Chancellor's fiscal rules are then tested against that projected revenue.

Lower the productivity assumption and the projected revenue falls with it. Analysis circulating ahead of the Budget suggests a downgrade of around 0.3 percentage points would open a hole in the region of £20 billion by 2029-30. Not because anything happened in the real economy that week. Because a forecast changed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is what most Budget commentary lacks. That £20 billion figure is a projection contingent on a downgrade the OBR has not yet published. It may be larger. It may be smaller. What is not in doubt is the direction of travel: forecasters have been marking down Britain's productivity potential for years, and there is no sign of that reversing before October.

The Three Taxes They Have Ruled Out — and the Ones They Haven't

The Government has said it will honour Labour's 2024 manifesto pledges not to raise the headline rates of income tax, VAT or National Insurance. Fine. Hold them to it. But understand what that commitment does and does not cover.

It does not cover thresholds. It does not cover allowances. It does not cover reliefs, duties, levies, business rates, capital gains, dividend rates, pension treatment, or the long list of charges that raise real money without ever appearing as a change to a rate people recognise. A promise not to raise three named taxes, made while a £20 billion gap opens elsewhere, is not a promise to leave your money alone. It is a promise about where the letters will not appear.

Frozen thresholds are the clearest example, and this site has covered them before. If your pay rises with inflation and the threshold does not move, you pay a higher share of your income in tax and nobody had to legislate a rate rise to do it. It is a tax increase that arrives disguised as a pay rise.

Where the Economy Actually Is

It is worth being straight about the backdrop rather than picking only the grim bits. Inflation was 2.6 per cent in the twelve months to June 2026, according to the ONS release on 22 July — well down from the peaks of recent years, though still above the Bank of England's two per cent target. The July figure is due on 19 August and will land before the Budget.

Government borrowing in June came in at around £16 billion. That was £7.9 billion lower than the same month a year earlier, and slightly below the OBR's own forecast of £16.3 billion. That is a better number than the Government's critics, including me, might have predicted, and it should be said plainly. Total debt, however, remains close to the annual value of the entire economy — which is why a forecast downgrade bites so hard. When the stock of debt is that large, small changes to projected growth translate into very large changes to what the Treasury believes it can afford.

So the picture is not one of imminent collapse. It is one of a country with almost no room for error, run by people who keep needing more room.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK would attack the problem at its source rather than at the till. The reason the productivity assumption keeps being cut is that Britain has spent two decades taxing work and enterprise heavily, regulating them thickly, and building almost nothing quickly. You cannot forecast your way out of that. You have to change it.

That means lifting the income tax threshold so that work pays before the state takes its cut, rather than freezing thresholds and calling it fiscal discipline. It means a serious attack on the cost of the regulatory state — the compliance burden that falls hardest on the small firms that would otherwise be hiring. It means cheap, reliable energy, because no manufacturing revival survives industrial electricity prices among the highest in the developed world.

And it means honesty about spending. A government that cannot say which programmes it will stop is a government that has already decided you will pay for all of them. Every Budget that closes a gap with stealth taxes rather than choices makes the next gap wider.

On 28 October, watch the thresholds, not the headlines. That is where the £20 billion will come from.
