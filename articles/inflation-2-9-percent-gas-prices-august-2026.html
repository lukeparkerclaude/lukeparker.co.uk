Inflation Climbs Back to 2.9% as Gas Prices Jump 14.7%: The Squeeze Is Not Over

Inflation rose to 2.9% in the year to July, the first increase since March. Gas prices are up 14.7% on the year. Whatever ministers say about turning a corner, the bills landing on doormats tell a different story.

There is a particular kind of insult in being told the economy is stabilising while your energy bill goes up. On Wednesday the Office for National Statistics published its inflation figures for July, and they made the point better than any politician could. Inflation rose to 2.9%, up from 2.6% in June — the first increase in the annual rate since March. The single biggest driver was the cost of keeping the lights on and the heating running.

The Numbers Behind the Headline

The Consumer Prices Index rose by 2.9% in the 12 months to July 2026, according to the ONS release published on 19 August. CPIH, the measure that includes owner occupiers' housing costs, rose to 3.1% from 2.8%. On the month alone, prices rose 0.3%, compared with 0.1% in the same month last year.

The ONS is explicit about where the pressure came from. Housing and household services — the category that contains your gas and electricity — jumped to an annual rate of 4.1%, up from 2.7% the month before. Within that, gas prices were up 14.7% on the year and electricity up 3.6%, reflecting the rise in the Ofgem price cap.

That is the whole story in one line. Inflation did not drift upwards because of some abstract global force. It went up because energy got more expensive, and energy got more expensive because of choices made in Westminster over more than a decade.

What Is Actually Getting Cheaper

It would be dishonest to pretend everything is going the wrong way, and I am not going to do that. Food and non-alcoholic drink inflation fell to 1.3%, down from 1.7% and the lowest rate since September 2021. Transport inflation eased to 3.6% from 5.7%, with motor fuel inflation coming down from 21.3% to 15.5%. Core inflation, which strips out energy and food, was unchanged at 2.6%.

So there are parts of the basket where the pressure is genuinely easing. But note what that tells you: the areas where government policy has the least direct grip are improving, and the area where government policy has the most direct grip is the one dragging the number back up. That is not bad luck. That is a policy failure with a postcode.

A £45 Sticking Plaster on a 14.7% Wound

The government's answer is to cut VAT on domestic electricity from 5% to zero from 1 October, a measure announced on 21 July and expected to take around £45 off the annual price cap at a cost of roughly £850 million this financial year. I will give credit where it is due: cutting a tax is better than raising one, and the Treasury's own estimate is that it will knock about 0.10 percentage points off CPI.

But look at the arithmetic honestly. Forty-five pounds off the annual bill, against gas prices that have risen 14.7% in a year. It applies to electricity but not to gas — and gas is the component that rose by almost fifteen per cent. It lasts for this financial year only. And it does nothing whatever about the reason bills are high in the first place.

Meanwhile the current price cap stands at £1,663 for a typical direct debit household, and Ofgem must announce the October cap by 26 August. Analysts at Cornwall Insight and E.ON have both been forecasting an increase. Families will find out within days whether the £45 is swallowed whole before it ever arrives.

The Bank of England's Target Is 2%

It is worth remembering, amid the commentary about inflation being "under control", that the Bank of England's target is 2%. We are at 2.9% on CPI and 3.1% on CPIH, and the direction of travel in July was upwards for the first time in four months. Every month above target is another month in which wages have to run harder simply to stand still.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK's position is that you cannot tax and subsidise your way out of an energy cost problem that you created through supply. We would scrap the net zero levies loaded onto household bills, exploit Britain's own gas reserves in the North Sea and onshore, and stop paying other countries to sell us energy we could produce here. A country sitting on its own energy should not be at the mercy of international gas prices, and British families should not be paying a premium for the privilege of importing what is under their feet.

Cutting VAT by £45 while gas rises 14.7% is not an energy policy. It is a press release. The people I represent in Preston East do not need another announcement. They need bills that come down and stay down — and that means producing more of our own energy, not taxing the symptom.

HM Treasury / DESNZ, New PM cuts tax on household electricity bills, published 21 July 2026 (VAT cut to 0% from 1 October, ~£45 saving, ~£850m cost, ~0.10pp CPI effect).
