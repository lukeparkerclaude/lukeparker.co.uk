Nobody in Lancashire Voted for a Mayor. So Let's Actually Ask Them.

Lancashire is the last part of the North without an elected mayor, and central government would very much like that to change. But nobody has asked the one and a half million people who live here. Reform UK's leader at County Hall is calling for a binding referendum before any council is abolished or any mayor created — and with reorganisation now paused, there is no excuse left for not asking.

There is a question hanging over Lancashire that nobody in Westminster wants to put to the people who actually live here: do you want an elected mayor?

Not "would you like the funding that comes with one". Not "wouldn't you rather not be left behind". The plain question. And so far, the answer from central government has been that you don't get to give one.

How We Got Here

Lancashire has a combined county authority — it launched in February 2025 — but it is a non-mayoral one. Members have been split ever since over whether to go further and take a directly elected mayor. Meanwhile the Deputy Prime Minister, Angela Rayner, declared that "by May 2026, the whole of the North will have mayors", which rather gives away the spirit of the thing.

The official line is more careful: government says it will not impose mayors on areas that do not want one, while making clear it considers a directly accountable mayor the strongest form of governance available. Read that twice. It is not a choice. It is a preference with the word "choice" written on the front of it.

Ask The People

Cllr Stephen Atkinson, who leads Reform UK at County Hall, has said he has assurances that a mayor is not mandatory, and he has launched a petition to ensure any change to the structure of local government in Lancashire is put to the public in a referendum.

That is not obstruction. That is the most basic democratic proposition there is. If you are going to abolish councils people have voted for, redraw the map, and create a brand new elected office with real power over transport, skills and planning, you ask them first. We are talking about permanently changing who governs one and a half million people. A consultation nobody reads is not consent.

And note the state of play: local government reorganisation has been paused while checks are carried out to make sure the process is lawful — a delay reported to have set things back at least 18 months. If there was ever a moment to stop, take a breath, and simply ask the public, it is now. The clock everyone was being rushed against has already stopped.

What This Really Costs

Let's be honest about the money too, because it matters. In February the county council set a net budget of £1,329.312 million for 2026-27, with council tax up 3.8% — 1.80% general plus a 2% adult social care precept — and it is built on £65 million of planned savings this year, with over £100 million more forecast to be needed across the following two years.

That is the real picture in Lancashire. Adult social care and SEND costs rising faster than any council can absorb. Highways that need every penny of the £72 million maintenance pot and more. And into that, we are being nudged towards a new tier of government with a new office, a new staff, and — as critics have warned — a new precept on your council tax bill to pay for it.

My honest read: I am not against devolution in principle. Powers exercised in Preston beat powers exercised in Whitehall almost every time. But there is a world of difference between bringing power closer to people and adding another layer of politician between them and the decisions.

Where Reform UK Stands

If it is genuinely local government, let local people decide. Reform UK's position in Lancashire is simple: no mayor, no abolition of councils and no redrawing of the map without a binding public vote first; full disclosure of what a mayoral precept would add to a Band D bill before anyone is asked to decide; and devolution judged on whether it actually improves potholes, buses, school places and social care — not on whether it earns Lancashire a seat at somebody else's table.

Being "the last area in the North without a mayor" is only an embarrassment if you think the point of local government is to match the neighbours. The point is to serve the people who pay for it. Ask them. If Lancashire votes yes, I will accept that without complaint. What I will not accept is a settlement imposed on this county by people who never put the question.
