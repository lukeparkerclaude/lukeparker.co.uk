62,199 Arrests for Things People Said Online. One in Five Ended in a Conviction.

A new Big Brother Watch report puts a number on something plenty of us already felt: at least 62,199 people arrested in five years over messages, posts and jokes, under laws written for malicious communications. Only 12,292 were convicted. In Cumbria you were more than forty times more likely to be arrested for it than in Staffordshire. That is not policing. That is a lottery.

Sixty-two thousand, one hundred and ninety-nine. That is how many people were arrested in this country over five years for what they communicated — messages, posts, comments, jokes — according to a new report from the civil liberties group Big Brother Watch.

Work it out and it comes to roughly 34 arrests every single day. Not 34 burglaries solved. Not 34 shoplifters stopped. Thirty-four people, every day, having a police officer turn up over something they typed.

The Numbers, Exactly As Reported

Big Brother Watch sent Freedom of Information requests to all 45 territorial police forces in the UK and got complete arrest data back from 38 of them. So the headline figure is a floor, not a ceiling — the real number is higher, and we do not know by how much, because seven forces could not or would not say.

The arrests were made between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2025 under three pieces of law: Section 1 of the Malicious Communications Act 1988, Section 127 of the Communications Act 2003, and Section 179 of the Online Safety Act 2023. Of the 62,199 arrested, 12,292 were convicted. That is about one in five.

Think about what that ratio actually means. Four out of every five people dragged into a police station over a message walked back out without a conviction. They still had the arrest. They still had the phone seized, the hours in a cell, the conversation with an employer, the entry on a record that follows them into every enhanced check for the rest of their life.

A Postcode Lottery for Your Own Mouth

Here is the part that should end the argument about whether this is being applied sensibly. Cumbria recorded 25.69 communications-offence arrests per 10,000 residents between 2021 and 2025. Staffordshire recorded 0.58. Northamptonshire was 23.35. Surrey was 1.70. Northumbria was 1.87.

That is a gap of more than forty to one between the top and the bottom. Big Brother Watch's point is the obvious one: differences on that scale cannot be explained by people in Cumbria simply being ruder than people in Staffordshire.

Let's be honest about what a spread like that tells you. It means there is no national standard. It means the same post can be a crime in one county and a shrug in the next, and the only variable is which force happens to cover your street. Whatever you call that, you cannot call it equality before the law.

Laws Written for One Thing, Used for Another

My honest read is that nobody set out to build this. The Malicious Communications Act was passed in 1988, before most of the people now being arrested under it were born. It was aimed at poison-pen letters and genuine threats — the man who sends a woman something vile through her door at midnight. Almost everyone agrees that should be a crime.

What happened is that the law stayed still and the world moved. Everything became a communication. Every argument in a pub became an argument in a comment section, in writing, permanently, in front of anyone who cared to screenshot it. And a piece of legislation designed for a narrow, nasty category of behaviour became a catch-all that can be pointed at almost anything anybody says.

Meanwhile, ask anyone who has had a bike stolen, a shed broken into or a car window put through what response they got. That is the comparison that stings. It is not that police have too few resources. It is that somewhere along the line the incentives were set so that a complaint about a post is easier to log, easier to action and easier to count than a burglary is to solve.

Where Reform UK Stands

Free speech is not a technicality. It is the thing that lets you complain about everything else. Reform UK's position is that the criminal law should deal with threats, harassment, incitement and the targeting of children — and should stop there. Being offensive is not the same as being dangerous, and a country that cannot tell the difference will keep arresting tens of thousands of people to secure a conviction rate of one in five.

What needs to happen is not complicated. Repeal or rewrite the communications offences so they cover threats and harassment rather than hurt feelings. Set one national standard so a Cumbrian and a Staffordshire man are treated the same. And make forces publish these arrests openly, every quarter, so nobody has to send 45 Freedom of Information requests to find out what is being done in their name.

Britain used to be the country other people pointed at when they wanted an example of free expression. Thirty-four arrests a day is not the record of a confident nation. It is the record of a nervous one.
