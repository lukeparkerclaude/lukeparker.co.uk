Your Right to a Jury Is on the Chopping Block. The Lords Must Hold the Line.

Peers started line-by-line scrutiny of the Courts and Tribunals Bill this week. Clause 1 would stop adult defendants charged with either-way offences from choosing trial by jury. Even the Lords' own Constitution Committee says changes to jury trial are of constitutional importance. My honest read: this is a backlog problem being solved by taking away a centuries-old freedom.

Trial by jury is one of the oldest protections an ordinary person has against the state. Twelve of your neighbours, not a government-appointed official, decide whether you are guilty. This week the House of Lords began going through, line by line, a Bill that would take a big chunk of that protection away.

What the Bill Actually Does

The Courts and Tribunals Bill began its Lords Committee stage on Tuesday 10 November, with more scrutiny scheduled for Monday 16 November. As the Lords Constitution Committee sets out, Clause 1 would stop adult defendants charged with "either-way" offences from electing a jury trial in the Crown Court. Clause 3 would allow judge-only trials in a range of complex or lengthy cases.

Either-way offences are not parking tickets. They are serious enough that, today, the defendant can choose to be tried by a jury. Under this Bill, that choice goes.

When the Bill went through a Commons vote in March, 304 MPs voted for it and 203 against. Ministers have already backed away from one part of the original plan: non-jury trials for either-way offences carrying up to three years in prison were dropped. But the core of it, ending the right to elect, is still there.

Even the Lords' Own Committees Are Worried

This isn't just campaigners talking. The Lords Constitution Committee said plainly: "Changes to trial by jury are of constitutional importance," and should only be made on a strong justification from the government. It urged peers to ask whether the measures are proportionate.

The Delegated Powers Committee went further. It wants two ministerial powers stripped out of the Bill altogether, including a power for ministers to add offences to the judge-only list, which it described as a "Henry VIII power". In plain English: a minister could widen the list of cases tried without a jury without Parliament going through a full Bill. That should worry anyone, whoever is in government.

Seventy-six amendments have been tabled, ten of them from four former judges and Lord Bellamy. When former judges are queueing up to amend your court reforms, you have a problem.

Fix the Backlog, Don't Fix the Jury

Let's be honest about why this is happening. The Crown Court backlog is a disgrace, and victims are waiting far too long for justice. But the answer is more court time, more sitting days, more judges and properly maintained courtrooms, not quietly removing a right that has protected ordinary people for centuries.

The Free Speech Union, which has campaigned against the Bill, estimates the reforms would cut jury trials by about a third, to around 10,000 a year. Whatever the exact figure, once a right like this is gone, it rarely comes back.

Where Reform UK Stands

Reform UK believes the right to be judged by your peers is not a bureaucratic inconvenience to be trimmed when the system gets slow. Fund the courts properly and clear the backlog, keep the right to elect a jury trial, and never hand ministers a power to expand judge-only trials by the back door. I hope peers of every party hold the line when they return to this Bill on Monday.
