Knife Crime Is Down and Shop Robbery Is Up 91%: Read the Crime Figures Properly

Police recorded 5.2 million crimes in the year to March 2026. The Crime Survey estimated 9.6 million incidents. Knife crime fell 8 per cent and homicide hit its lowest level since 2003. Shop robbery rose 91 per cent. Anyone quoting only one of those numbers is selling you something.

Crime statistics are the most abused numbers in British politics. Every party has a favourite table and every party quotes it as though the others do not exist. So let me do the unfashionable thing and give you the whole set from the Office for National Statistics for the year ending March 2026, including the parts that do not suit my argument.

The Numbers That Went Down

Offences involving a knife or sharp instrument fell by 8 per cent, to 48,774, from 53,096 the previous year. Homicides fell by 7 per cent, to 499 — the lowest figure since current police recording practices began in 2003. Shoplifting fell by around 4 per cent, to 507,086 offences, after reaching an all-time high the year before.

Those are real falls in serious categories, and I am not going to squint at them until they look like something else. If a Reform politician had been in the Home Office and produced a homicide figure that was the lowest in twenty-three years, we would be shouting about it. Consistency requires acknowledging it when someone else does.

So: on the headline violent categories, the direction of travel in the year to March 2026 was downward. That is the honest starting point for any argument about crime in Britain, including mine.

The Numbers That Went Up

Now the rest of the table. Robberies of businesses and shops in England and Wales jumped by 91 per cent — a rise the ONS attributes substantially to changes in how such offences are recorded, which had previously left violence against shop staff hidden inside other categories. Personal theft rose by 22 per cent.

The recording-change caveat matters and I have given it to you up front. But think about what it actually means. The violence was always happening. We simply were not counting it. The shopkeeper in Preston who was threatened behind her own counter did not experience a recording methodology. She experienced a robbery that, until the rules changed, the national statistics effectively did not see.

That is not a reason to distrust the ONS, which changed the rules precisely in order to see it. It is a reason to be careful about any long-run trend line in a category where the definition has moved.

The Gap That Explains the Politics

Here is the figure that does more work than any other. Police in England and Wales recorded around 5.2 million crimes in the year to March 2026. The Crime Survey for England and Wales, which asks the public directly about what has happened to them rather than relying on reports, estimated around 9.6 million incidents.

Roughly four and a half million incidents that people experienced never became a recorded crime. That gap is the whole argument. It is why a government can present a table of falling offences and be met by a public that is convinced things are getting worse — and why both sides can be telling the truth about their own evidence.

People stop reporting when they conclude that reporting achieves nothing. The bike is gone, the phone is gone, the shed was done over, and the realistic prospect of anyone attending, let alone charging someone, is close to zero. Once that belief sets in, recorded crime can fall for reasons that have nothing to do with a safer country.

I hear this constantly in Preston East, and I suspect every councillor in the country hears the same thing: what's the point? Three words that no crime statistic captures and that ought to worry the Home Office more than any of them.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK would judge policing on outcomes the public can actually feel, starting with charge rates rather than recorded volumes. A falling number of recorded offences alongside a static charge rate is not success — it is a public that has given up on you.

We would put officers back into visible, local, foot-based policing, because presence prevents offences that no detection rate ever recovers. We would treat retail crime and violence against shop workers as the serious offences the new recording rules have finally exposed them to be, rather than as a shrinkage problem for businesses to absorb. And we would end the practice of screening out whole categories of low-value theft without investigation, which is the single most efficient way to teach a community that reporting is pointless.

On sentencing, the argument is simple: prison places should exist in sufficient number that the sentence a judge hands down is the sentence served. A justice system that releases people early because it has run out of room is not making a decision about justice. It is making a decision about logistics, and calling it policy.

The falls in knife crime and homicide are genuine and welcome. The four-and-a-half-million-incident gap between what happens and what gets recorded is also genuine, and it is where the public's anger lives. A government serious about crime would be talking about both. This one has been notably quiet about the second.
