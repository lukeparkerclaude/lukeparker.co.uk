Parliament Has Gone Home for a Month. Your Bills Haven't.

The Commons rose for recess on 16 September. The Lords rise today. Neither House returns until 11 October. In between, on 1 October, the energy price cap goes up 4% — with nobody in the chamber to be asked about it. Then MPs come back for just over a fortnight before a Budget that will reshape household finances. Recess is normal. The timing of this one deserves a harder look.

Parliament has packed up. The Commons rose on 16 September. The Lords rise today, 18 September. Neither House sits again until 11 October.

Nearly four weeks. And look at what happens while the lights are off.

The Empty Chamber Calendar

On 1 October, Ofgem's price cap rises 4% to £1,723 for a typical dual-fuel household — about £60 a year more, with gas costs up 8%. That is the single most-felt policy event of the autumn for most families, and it lands in the middle of the recess. No urgent question. No statement. No minister at the despatch box explaining it to the country.

Then the Houses return on 11 October — and the Budget follows on 28 October. Just over a fortnight of sitting time between MPs walking back through the door and a Chancellor, John Healey, setting the tax and spending framework for the year ahead. A Budget that Andy Burnham has pointedly declined to rule out tax rises in.

Let's Be Honest About Recess

I am not going to tell you recess is a scandal, because it isn't, and anyone who says otherwise is playing you.

September recess exists partly for party conference season, and that is not some Westminster indulgence — it is one of the few points in the year when party members, including mine, get to actually shape what their party does. More importantly, MPs out of Westminster are generally MPs in their constituencies: surgeries, casework, schools, businesses, the bread-and-butter work that never gets a headline. As a county councillor I can tell you the work that matters most is rarely done in a chamber.

And recess dates are set well in advance. Nobody at Number 10 scheduled the price cap to dodge scrutiny — Ofgem's cap changes on fixed quarterly dates and always has.

So no conspiracy. But no excuse either.

The Accountability Gap

Here is my honest read. The problem is not that MPs get time in their constituencies. The problem is the sequencing — and the fact that nobody in charge seems to think the sequencing is a problem.

We have a Prime Minister who took office on 20 July 2026 without a general election. We have a Chancellor nobody voted for in that job. We have a Budget on 28 October that will raise money from somewhere. And between now and then, the Commons will sit for a fraction of the time the decisions are actually being taken.

Government does not stop for recess. Decisions get made, briefings get given, positions get locked in — all of it in the weeks when there is no floor of the House to answer for any of it. By the time MPs are back and up to speed, the Budget is a fortnight out and largely written.

Scrutiny that arrives after the decision is not scrutiny. It is commentary.

Where Reform UK Stands

Accountability should be built around the decisions, not around the diary. If the single biggest cost-of-living event of the autumn falls inside recess, the House should be recalled or the calendar should move. One of the two. Treating the parliamentary timetable as a fixed object that the country must work around has it exactly backwards.

And the deeper point stands. A Parliament elected in July 2024, under a leader who has since gone, now supports a Prime Minister and a Chancellor this country has never been asked about — and it is about to nod through a Budget on that basis. Whatever you call that, you cannot really call it a mandate.

The bills arriving on 1 October will not wait for Parliament to come back. Neither should the questions.
