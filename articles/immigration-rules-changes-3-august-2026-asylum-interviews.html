The New Immigration Rules Took Effect on 3 August. They Still Don't Go Far Enough

A package of Immigration Rules changes came into force on 3 August, including a merged asylum interview designed to decide weak claims faster. Some of it is genuinely sensible. All of it is years overdue, and none of it touches the fundamentals.

On 3 August a substantial package of amendments to the Immigration Rules came into force. It attracted almost no coverage, which is a shame, because buried inside it are changes that touch how Britain decides who may stay and who may not.

What Actually Changed

The amendments affect the Graduate route, applications involving children, Appendix FM covering family migration, immigration bail, asylum claims, Electronic Travel Authorisations, and the criminality provisions used to refuse or curtail leave.

The most consequential change concerns asylum interviews. From 3 August the Home Office may use a single merged interview to register a claim, ask screening questions, and put the substantive questions — why the applicant is claiming asylum, why they need protection, and why they should not be removed from the UK. The department says this may be used where it believes a claim can be decided quickly, including claims it considers “clearly unfounded”.

Credit Where It Is Due

I am going to say something that will surprise readers who expect a Reform councillor to condemn everything a Labour Home Office does: the merged interview is, in principle, the right idea.

The single greatest driver of cost in the asylum system is delay. Every month a claim sits undecided is another month of accommodation, support and legal expense, and the National Audit Office has documented in detail where that leads. If a claim is genuinely without merit, deciding it in one sitting rather than three is better for the taxpayer, better for the system, and better for genuine refugees whose cases are stuck behind it.

Refugee organisations have raised concerns about the pace of the process. On 6 August the Helen Bamber Foundation and Asylum Aid argued that recent immigration legislation has entrenched and expanded the criminalisation of survivors. Those concerns deserve to be recorded and taken seriously, even by those of us who want the system tightened considerably. A fast decision that is wrong helps nobody, and it will be overturned later at greater expense.

The Fundamentals Are Untouched

Here is the problem with the package as a whole. It is procedural. It adjusts how claims are handled once someone is already in the country and already in the system. It does not address the questions that actually determine the scale of that system.

It does not resolve how many people arrive. It does not resolve the interaction between our domestic law and the international conventions that make removal so difficult. And it does nothing about the enforcement gap at the end of the process — the point at which a claim is refused, all appeals are exhausted, and the person nonetheless remains.

The courts continue to work through these questions in parallel. On 12 August the Court of Appeal considered what counts as facilitation under the Withdrawal Agreement, a reminder that a great deal of British immigration policy is still being settled by judges interpreting agreements Parliament signed rather than by Parliament itself.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK would go considerably further than tidying interview procedure. We would set a statutory time limit for asylum decisions measured in weeks, backed by the caseworkers required to meet it. We would ensure that where a claim is refused and appeals are exhausted, removal actually follows — because a decision that is never enforced is not a decision at all.

And we would end the position in which the most consequential immigration questions are resolved by litigation rather than by the elected House of Commons. A merged interview is a sensible administrative tweak. It is not a border policy, and it should not be sold as one.
