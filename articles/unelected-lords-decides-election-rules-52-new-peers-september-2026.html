On Monday, an Unelected House Starts Deciding How You Vote

On Monday the Bill that lowers the voting age to 16, rewrites the rules on political donations and hands the Electoral Commission new powers begins its passage through the House of Lords. Fifty-two people were given seats in that chamber this year: 26 announced in May, and 26 more announced on 16 July by a Prime Minister who left office four days later. My honest read: you cannot fix the franchise in a chamber that has no franchise at all.

Tomorrow the Representation of the People Bill begins its journey through the House of Lords. This is the Bill that lowers the voting age to 16, changes the rules on who may fund a political party and how, and expands the Electoral Commission's enforcement powers. It is, in plain terms, the Bill that decides how you choose a government.

Not one of the people about to debate it was elected by you.

Fifty-Two Seats in One Year

Look at how the membership of that chamber has been topped up in 2026 alone.

On 12 May, 26 individuals who had previously sat as excepted hereditary peers were handed life peerages, allowing them to return to the Lords — 15 Conservatives, nine crossbenchers and two Labour. Then on 16 July, a further 26 new peers were announced, including the outgoing Mayor of London, Sadiq Khan: 16 Labour, five Liberal Democrats, three Conservatives and two crossbenchers.

That second list was published four days before Keir Starmer's premiership ended on 20 July. A Prime Minister on his way out of the door, with no mandate left to speak of, populating the chamber that will now scrutinise the rules of the next election. Let's be honest: in any other walk of life we would have a word for that, and it would not be "scrutiny".

Credit Where It Is Due

I will give the Government this. Removing the remaining hereditary peers, through the House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Act 2026, was a real reform and a defensible one. Inheriting a seat in the legislature belonged to a different century. The upper House now consists of life peers and the Lords Spiritual.

But look what actually happened next. Twenty-six of those hereditaries were promptly converted into life peers and sent straight back in. The principle was retired; the people were retained. And every one of the seats created since has been created by patronage — which is to say, by the gift of whoever happened to be Prime Minister that week.

Swapping an accident of birth for a favour from the Prime Minister is not democracy. It is a different flavour of the same problem.

The Part That Should Worry You

Here is my honest read on the week ahead. The Lords may well do some useful things to this Bill. It may be the chamber that finally imposes a real cap on political donations — the thing the Commons conspicuously declined to do for domestic donors. Peers will table sensible amendments, and some of them will improve the drafting. That is genuinely how the place is supposed to work.

But hold the two halves together. A Parliament elected in July 2024, on a manifesto the country has moved on from, under a Prime Minister nobody has voted for, is changing who gets to vote. And the revising chamber doing the checking is appointed, not elected, and was topped up 52 times this year by the very people whose rules are under review.

At no point in that chain does anybody have to ask you what you think.

Where Reform UK Stands

If you want to change the rules of the game, you need a mandate from the people who have to live under them. Reform UK opposed lowering the voting age, and I still think that was right — 18 is the age of majority in this country, and it should mean the same thing consistently.

On the Lords, my position is straightforward. An appointed chamber cannot be the last line of defence for democratic legitimacy, because it has none of its own to lend. Stop the patronage lists. Cap the size of the chamber. Move to an elected or at minimum properly independently-appointed second House, and let it earn its authority rather than inherit it from whoever is in Downing Street.

And in the meantime: call a general election. Let the country actually choose a government, and then let that government change the franchise with the authority to do it. Anything else is the establishment rearranging the rules while nobody is allowed a vote on any of it.
