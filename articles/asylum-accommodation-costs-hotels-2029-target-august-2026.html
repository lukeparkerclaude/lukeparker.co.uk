The Asylum Accommodation Bill: 144 Pounds a Night and a 2029 Promise That Keeps Slipping

A hotel bed for an asylum seeker costs the taxpayer 144 pounds a night. A dispersal bed costs 23.25 pounds. The National Audit Office says the contracts are running more than three times over their original estimate, and the alternatives cost more than the hotels they replace.

Two numbers explain most of what is wrong with Britain's asylum accommodation system. The Home Office's own estimate of the average cost of housing an asylum seeker is 144 pounds per person per night in a hotel. In dispersal accommodation — ordinary housing in ordinary communities — the figure is 23.25 pounds. The same person. The same duty of care. Six times the price.

Thirty Thousand People, Two Hundred Hotels

As of April 2026, around 30,000 people were still living in almost 200 hotels, at a cost to the taxpayer of roughly 8 million pounds a day. That is not a rounding error in a departmental budget. That is a sum of money that would fund a great deal of what my constituents in Preston East are repeatedly told cannot be afforded.

To be fair to the Home Office, the direction of travel has improved. Officials confirmed the closure of 11 asylum hotels in April 2026, the first large-scale withdrawal from accommodation that expanded rapidly during the pandemic. They say numbers in hotels have fallen by 20 per cent over the past year, and that those particular closures will save around 45 million pounds annually.

I will state that plainly because it is true and because accuracy matters more than point-scoring. But hold the figure up against the scale of the problem. Forty-five million pounds a year, set against a hotel bill running at roughly 8 million pounds a day. The saving amounts to less than a week of the total spend.

The Auditors Are Not Impressed

The National Audit Office has examined these contracts and its findings should have caused a great deal more political trouble than they did. Asylum accommodation contracts originally estimated at 4.5 billion pounds are now expected to cost 15.3 billion pounds over ten years. That is not overspending at the margins. That is a projection that has more than tripled.

Worse, the NAO found that the Home Office's plans for large-scale alternative accommodation — the sites promoted to the public as the sensible, cheaper answer to hotels — will in fact cost more than the hotels they were meant to replace, while delivering fewer places than planned.

The Committee scrutiny that followed also established how lopsided the spending is. People housed in hotels have accounted for around 35 per cent of everyone in asylum accommodation but roughly 76 per cent of the annual cost of the contracts. A third of the people, three quarters of the bill.

A Deadline Is Not a Policy

The Government's response is a commitment to end the use of asylum hotels by 2029, which then-Chancellor Rachel Reeves said would save around 1 billion pounds a year. Set aside for a moment whether the saving is realistic. Consider the timeline.

Twenty twenty-nine is three years away and comfortably beyond the point at which this Government must face the electorate. It is the classic Whitehall manoeuvre: convert an urgent problem into a distant target, bank the headline today, and leave the delivery to whoever inherits the department. Meanwhile the meter runs at 8 million pounds a day.

There is a second cost that never appears in the accounts. Every one of these hotels sits in someone's town. Local authorities are frequently informed rather than consulted, and residents get no say at all in a decision that reshapes their high street. That is not a fringe grievance. It is a straightforward failure of democratic accountability, and pretending otherwise has done enormous damage to public trust.

What Reform UK Would Do

The hotel bill is a symptom. The disease is a system that cannot decide claims quickly. Every week a case sits undetermined is another week of accommodation costs, and the NAO figures show precisely where that leads.

Reform UK would put the resource into decision-making rather than accommodation: process claims in weeks rather than years, grant status promptly to those genuinely entitled to it, and remove promptly those who are not. We would end the use of hotels immediately rather than in 2029, and we would renegotiate contracts that the Government's own auditors have found to be delivering poor value on a spectacular scale.

And we would give local communities a statutory voice before accommodation decisions are imposed on them. None of this requires new legislation or new money. It requires a Government willing to treat 15.3 billion pounds of taxpayers' money as if it belonged to the taxpayer.
