Clacton Delivers Its Verdict: Farage Returned With 63% While The Establishment Parties Stayed Away

Nigel Farage resigned his seat and asked his own voters to judge him. On 13 August they returned him with 63.3% of the vote and a majority of 12,784 — against a field of 34 candidates and with no Labour or Conservative challenger on the ballot.

On the evening of 13 August, in a seaside town that the Westminster commentariat has spent two years patronising, the returning officer read out a number that settled the argument. Nigel Farage: 22,239 votes. A 63.3% share. A majority of 12,784. And in second place, on 26.9%, a man in a bin.

You can laugh at the optics. Plenty of national newspapers did. But look past the novelty candidates and the record-breaking ballot paper, and Clacton has just done something the political class did not want to happen: it gave a straight answer to a straight question.

Why There Was A By-Election At All

Farage announced his resignation as MP for Clacton on 7 July, and it was accepted the following day. He did so amid parliamentary scrutiny of his personal finances, including allegations concerning undeclared gifts — most prominently a £5 million payment from the Thailand-based businessman Christopher Harborne. Farage has dismissed the affair as an “establishment smear”.

What he did next is the part worth noticing. He did not sit tight and wait for a committee to grind through its process. He did not brief anonymously against his critics. He resigned the seat and put himself back in front of the only people whose verdict actually binds him: his own constituents.

That is not a small thing in modern British politics. Members of Parliament have clung on through considerably worse. The recall petition system exists precisely because so many refuse to go voluntarily. Farage removed the question of whether he should be forced out by choosing to be judged instead.

The Parties That Did Not Turn Up

Thirty-four candidates stood in Clacton — believed to be the most in any UK parliamentary election, surpassing the 26 who contested Haltemprice and Howden in 2008. And yet the ballot paper had a hole in the middle of it, because the established parties declined to field a serious challenge.

Think about what that means. A sitting MP resigns under a cloud of allegations. A by-election is called. And the parties that spend their days telling the country Farage is a danger to it could not bring themselves to put a candidate in front of the voters and make that argument.

If Reform UK really were the threat they describe in their press releases, Clacton was the open goal. Instead they stayed in the dressing room and left the pitch to Count Binface. You cannot simultaneously claim a man is unfit for public life and refuse to contest the seat he has just vacated. Voters notice that sort of thing. They noticed it here.

What The Numbers Actually Say

Let us be honest about the figures rather than gilding them, because the honest version is strong enough. Farage won Clacton in 2024 with 46.2% of the vote. On Thursday he took 63.3% — a substantial increase in share, and just over a thousand more votes in raw terms than he received two years ago.

Turnout was 44.4%. That is respectable for an August by-election, but it is well short of a general election, and anyone claiming this as a national verdict is overreaching. A by-election in one Essex constituency in the middle of the summer holidays is not a referendum on the government. It is a local judgement by local people on their local MP.

But it is a judgement, and it was emphatic. Nearly two-thirds of those who voted backed a man the national media had spent a fortnight writing off. Count Binface’s 26.9% was his best ever parliamentary result and reflects, in part, voters with nowhere else to go. That is the establishment parties’ failure, not Reform’s.

The Lesson Westminster Will Try Not To Learn

There is a habit in British politics of treating places like Clacton as a problem to be explained rather than a constituency to be represented. Coastal, working-class, unfashionable, overlooked by every government of every colour for forty years. The people who live there are perfectly capable of assessing a politician’s conduct and reaching their own view.

They did that on Thursday. They were given every reason by the national press to withdraw support, and they declined. Whether you like the outcome or not, that is democracy functioning exactly as intended: a representative submitted himself for judgement, and the people judged.

The investigations into Farage’s declarations continue, and they should be allowed to run their course properly and publicly — nobody in politics should be above scrutiny, including him. But the question of whether Clacton still wants him as its MP has been asked and answered, by the only jury that gets a vote on it.

The parties that refused to stand might reflect on why they were so confident of the argument and so frightened of the ballot.
