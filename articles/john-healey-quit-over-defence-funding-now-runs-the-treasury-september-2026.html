He Resigned Over the 3% Defence Target. Now, as Chancellor, He's Shelved It.

In June, John Healey resigned as Defence Secretary because the Treasury 'has been unwilling to commit the resources that the nation needs to defend the country.' Five weeks later he was made Chancellor of that same Treasury. And now? He has shelved the 3%-of-GDP defence target he resigned over. The man who quit on principle is the man who just broke it.

Every so often a story exposes the whole Westminster game in a single sequence of events. This is one of them — and it now has an ending.

Act One: The Principled Resignation

On 11 June, John Healey resigned as Defence Secretary. His letter was damning. He wrote that the Prime Minister had been "unable, and the Treasury has been unwilling, to commit the resources that the nation needs to defend the country at this time of rising threats." He warned that the existing path — drifting towards just 2.7% of GDP by 2030 — would leave Britain exposed, and said he was being forced into decisions that "could make the country less safe." The military had asked for around £28 billion; the Government offered roughly £13.5 billion. Healey walked. The Armed Forces Minister walked with him.

Act Two: The Punchline

Five weeks later, Andy Burnham became Prime Minister and appointed John Healey Chancellor of the Exchequer — head of the very Treasury he had just blamed for making the country "less safe." The block he resigned over became his own office, his own signature, his own decision. The excuse evaporated overnight.

Act Three: The Climbdown

So what has the man who resigned over defence funding done now that he controls the money? He has shelved the target he resigned over. The Treasury has confirmed that Healey's first Budget, on 28 October, will not commit to spending 3% of GDP on defence by 2030 — the decision is instead kicked down the road to a spending review in 2027.

Read that back slowly. In June, as Defence Secretary, he said the failure to fund defence properly could make the country less safe, and he quit over it. In October, as Chancellor, he is declining to commit the very money he said was essential. Same man. Same target. Opposite decision — and this time, no one else to blame.

Principle, or Positioning?

There are only two ways to read this. Either the danger he warned of in June was real — in which case the Chancellor is now knowingly leaving the country exposed by his own definition — or the resignation was never really about the money at all. It was a well-timed exit that made him look principled at exactly the moment a leadership contest was brewing, and delivered him the second most powerful job in the country weeks later. Either way, the Armed Forces are the ones left short.

Where Reform UK Stands

The defence of the realm is the first duty of any government — not a bargaining chip in a leadership manoeuvre. Reform UK would fund our Armed Forces to meet the threats we actually face, and we would not insult the public by resigning over defence money one month and shelving it the next. John Healey told the country its safety depended on hitting 3% by 2030. He now holds the cheque book — and he has put the pen down. He has run clean out of people to blame. The only question left is whether anyone in Westminster still remembers what a resignation on principle is actually supposed to mean.
