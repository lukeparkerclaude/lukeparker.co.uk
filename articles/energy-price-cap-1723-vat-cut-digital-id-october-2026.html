Energy Bills Up Again to £1,723, and the VAT Cut Barely Touches It

From 1 October the Ofgem price cap rose to £1,723 for a typical household, up 4% and a three-year high. The Government's answer is a six-month VAT cut on electricity worth about £45 a year. It's a real saving, and I'll say so. But it's a plaster on a problem the Government isn't touching.

Let me give credit where it's due first. From 1 October, VAT on household electricity fell from 5% to 0%. It runs for six months, to 31 March 2027, and suppliers apply it automatically, including for customers on fixed tariffs. Ofgem says that without it the headline figure would have been about £45 higher for a typical household. Forty-five pounds is forty-five pounds. I'm not going to sneer at it.

But let's look at the number it is being applied to.

The Cap: £1,723

Ofgem announced on 26 August that the price cap for a typical dual-fuel household rises to £1,723 a year from 1 October, up £60 (4%) from £1,663. That is the highest level in three years. Electricity bills rise by around 1%, gas by around 8%. Ofgem blames "higher wholesale gas prices as a result of the ongoing conflict in the Middle East." One caveat Ofgem itself makes: it is a cap on unit rates and standing charges, "not a cap on your total bill." Use more, pay more.

So the headline is this: after the Government's tax cut, a typical bill is still up £60. Without the cut it would be up around £105. The cut softens the blow. It does not reverse it.

Gas Households Get Less

Here is a detail worth knowing. The VAT cut applies to electricity only. Gas stays at 5% VAT, and gas is the fuel going up by 8%. Millions of Lancashire homes heat with gas. So the fuel rising fastest is the one that gets no relief. My view: if cutting VAT on energy is right for electricity, ask why it isn't right for gas.

Who Pays for It?

Prime Minister Andy Burnham announced the cut on his second day in office, on 21 July. According to ITV News, it costs the Exchequer around £850 million in 2026/27 and is paid for by scrapping the Digital ID programme, which ITV reported was expected to cost £600 million. That leaves a gap of roughly £250 million against the £850 million cost. Scrapping Digital ID is a decision I welcome, but the sums don't add up on their own, and someone will be asked to cover the difference. Martin Lewis, as ITV reported, warned that with the cap then predicted to rise 3.1% in October, the saving would be less noticeable. The cap has since come in at a 4% rise.

Where Reform UK Stands

Families shouldn't need a six-month tax holiday to get through an energy bill. Reform UK's argument is that energy policy should put affordable, reliable supply first, and that the layers of cost loaded onto household bills should be challenged, not accepted. A six-month VAT cut is welcome as far as it goes. But it expires in March, the cap is at a three-year high, and gas households are left out. Britain needs a plan to bring bills down for good, not a temporary discount that disappears just when the cold weather is at its worst.
