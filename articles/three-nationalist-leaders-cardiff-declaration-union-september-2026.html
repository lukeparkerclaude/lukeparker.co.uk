Three Nationalist Leaders, One Declaration, and a Union Nobody Is Defending

On Monday in Cardiff, the leaders of the SNP, Plaid Cymru and Sinn Fein signed a joint declaration demanding the right to hold independence referendums. It triggers nothing on its own. But it is the first time all three have moved together, and the government's only answer so far is that a vote is 'off limits' for now.

Something happened in Cardiff this week that deserves more attention than it got. John Swinney of the SNP, Rhun ap Iorwerth of Plaid Cymru and Michelle O'Neill of Sinn Fein met and signed a joint declaration demanding the right to self-determination and the right to hold independence referendums in their respective countries.

Reports indicate the declaration ranges across the economy, energy, Europe and cooperation between them, as well as self-determination. Notably, the three met in their capacity as party leaders rather than as officeholders in their respective administrations. That distinction matters legally. It matters far less politically.

What It Does and Doesn't Do

Let's be honest about the mechanics, because plenty of people will overstate this in both directions. The declaration does not trigger a referendum anywhere. It has no legal force. Constitutional matters remain reserved to Westminster, and no amount of signing documents in Cardiff changes that.

What it does is signal coordinated intent, and it keeps the pressure on at a moment when London has closed the door. Andy Burnham is reported to have written to Swinney making clear that a Scottish independence referendum is off limits for the time being. That is the entire government position, as far as anyone can tell: not now.

'Not Now' Is Not an Argument

Here is my honest read. For the first time, pro-independence parties lead in all three of the UK's other nations simultaneously. That is not an accident and it is not a blip. It is what happens when the Union is administered rather than argued for.

For years the establishment answer to nationalism has been procedural. Block the vote, question the mandate, run down the clock. Nobody in Westminster seems willing to stand up and make a positive case that Britain is worth keeping together. You cannot defeat an argument about identity and self-government with a legal technicality. You defeat it by being a country people actually want to belong to.

And that is where the real failure sits. If you want people in Glasgow, Cardiff and Belfast to choose Britain, Britain has to deliver something. Secure borders. Affordable energy. Public services that work. Money that stays in communities rather than being cycled through Whitehall and handed back as a grant with strings attached. Instead, people across every part of this country have watched living standards stagnate and been told to be grateful.

Where Reform UK Stands

Reform UK is unambiguously a party of the Union. We believe in the United Kingdom, all four nations of it, and we are not embarrassed to say so.

But defending the Union means more than saying no to referendums. It means giving people in every part of Britain a reason to want in. That means taking control of our borders, cutting the cost of energy by using our own resources, cutting the waste that eats tax revenue before it reaches a single frontline service, and pushing real decisions and real money down to communities instead of concentrating both in Westminster.

Nationalists in Cardiff, Edinburgh and Belfast are making their case openly and in concert. The least the government could do is make one back.
