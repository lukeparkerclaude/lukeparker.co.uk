Free Speech Gets Its Day in the Commons on Friday. Will Labour Finally Turn Up?

On Friday, MPs debate a Bill to stop wealthy claimants using the threat of crippling legal action to silence reporters and campaigners. The government says it is 'committed' to legislating. It has been saying that for a long time.

There is a quiet way to shut someone up in Britain. You don't need to arrest them. You just need deep pockets and a good law firm. Threaten to sue, drag it out, and watch the journalist, campaigner or local blogger back down because they simply can't afford to fight.

These are called SLAPPs — Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation. And on Friday 27 November, MPs get a chance to do something about them.

What's Happening on Friday

The first Bill up on Friday's Private Members' Bill day is the Strategic Litigation Against Public Participation (No. 2) Bill, brought by Sir John Whittingdale. It aims, in the words of the Hansard Society, "to curb the use of litigation, or the threat of it, that would silence reporting and campaigning in the public interest." A parallel Bill was introduced in the Lords by Baroness Stowell in June.

The idea is simple. Someone facing an abusive claim can ask the court to throw it out early. The court weighs the public interest in free speech against the claim's real chances of success. If it is thrown out, the claimant pays the defendant's costs.

The Gap in the Law

Some protection already exists, in the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 — but it only applies to cases relating to economic crime. If you are being threatened over anything else, you are on your own. As Baroness Stowell put it: "While some protection against SLAPPs was introduced in the last Parliament, it only covers economic crime."

Not everyone agrees a new law is needed. Some media lawyers argue that "provisions already exist to strike out claims that clearly lack merit or are abusive." That is a fair point to test at Second Reading. But my honest read is that when the threat of a six-figure legal bill is enough to kill a story before it is written, the courts' existing powers are not reaching the people who need them.

Promises, Promises

Labour has pledged to act on SLAPPs. It has not yet done so. The Free Speech Union says "Ministers have repeatedly failed to tackle the issue despite committing to do so." On 10 November, Courts Minister Sarah Sackman said ministers remained committed to legislating and that she would set out the government's position at Second Reading.

So Friday is the test. Either the government backs a workable Bill, or it explains why the powerful should keep their favourite weapon a little longer.

Where Reform UK Stands

Free speech is not a luxury for people who can afford lawyers. Reform UK stands for the right of ordinary people — local reporters, campaigners, residents asking awkward questions of those in power — to speak without being bullied into silence through the courts. Whoever is in government, if a reasonable, cross-party Bill protects that right, it should be backed. Labour has had its warm words. On Friday it needs to show up.
