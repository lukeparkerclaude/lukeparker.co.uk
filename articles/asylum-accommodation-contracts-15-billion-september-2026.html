£4.5 Billion Became £15.3 Billion. Nobody Lost Their Job Over It.

Britain signed asylum accommodation contracts estimated at £4.5 billion. They are now forecast to cost £15.3 billion over ten years — more than triple. Hotels took 76% of the annual spend to house 35% of the people. The hotel population is falling, which is welcome. The cost is not. And the 2026 break clause is the one chance to stop signing the same deal again.

Here is a number that ought to end a few careers. The contracts the Home Office signed to house asylum seekers were originally estimated at a total cost of £4.5 billion. They are now estimated to cost £15.3 billion over ten years.

That is not a rounding error. That is not inflation. That is more than three times the original figure, on contracts the state wrote itself, with terms the state chose, signed by people who are still in post.

Where the Money Actually Went

The detail is worse than the headline. In 2024/25, hotel accommodation accounted for 76% of the annual cost of the asylum contracts — while housing only 35% of the people in asylum accommodation.

Read that twice. Three quarters of the money went on the option that covered barely a third of the need. If a council in Lancashire ran a service like that, the auditors would be in the building by Friday.

And this is the honest part, which I am going to state plainly because I would rather be accurate than convenient: the number of people in hotels is falling. At the end of December 2025 hotels housed 30,657 people. That is down 19% on 2024, and 45% below the peak of 56,018 in September 2023. Anyone telling you hotel use is exploding right now is not reading the figures.

But falling occupancy and falling cost are not the same thing. The forecast on these contracts went the other way. You can empty hotels and still be paying a fortune, if the deal you signed guarantees the provider gets paid either way.

Contracts Written by People Who Will Never Have to Live With Them

The National Audit Office and the Home Affairs Committee have been blunt about why. The Home Office used large-scale contracts with private providers that gave it few levers to control costs or to force providers to deliver what was actually required. And then, having written contracts with no grip in them, it neglected the day-to-day management of those contracts too.

So: weak terms, then weak oversight of the weak terms. The providers did what any company does when handed a guaranteed customer with no ability to walk away. They took the money.

One contract has already gone. The Home Office confirmed the cancellation of Stay Belvedere Hotels' contract — worth around £2 billion a year — after a review raised concerns about performance, with the contract ending at the earliest opportunity in September 2026. That is this month — one provider finally cut loose, long after the ten-year forecast had already more than tripled.

The Break Clause Is Now

Here is why this matters today rather than at some vague point in the future. These contracts have a break clause in 2026 and run to 2029. That break clause is the single real piece of leverage the British state has over this system, and it exists right now.

Let's be honest about what usually happens with a moment like this. It gets studied. A review is commissioned. Officials explain that renegotiating would be disruptive, that continuity matters, that the market is thin. The clause passes unused, the contracts roll on to 2029, and in three years' time a committee writes another report saying much the same thing with a bigger number on the front.

My honest read is that the Burnham government will not use it properly, because using it means admitting the whole model was wrong rather than merely badly managed. A government that has spent its first weeks cutting VAT on electricity to save £45 a household is not going to enjoy explaining why the same Treasury signed off a £10.8 billion overrun without anyone being sacked.

Where Reform UK Stands

The scandal here is not only the money. It is that nobody carries the cost of getting it this wrong. Reform UK's position is that a state which cannot run a procurement process has no business lecturing anyone about value for money. The break clause should be used, not admired. The contracts should be rewritten with real penalties for underperformance, so that failure lands on the provider rather than the taxpayer. And the accommodation bill should be published in full, every quarter, per person, per provider, so the public can see what they are buying.

Underneath all of it sits the point Reform has made since the beginning: you do not fix the accommodation bill by getting better at accommodating people. You fix it by controlling the border, processing claims in weeks rather than years, and removing those with no right to be here. Every month a claim sits undecided is another month of hotel invoices — and on current contracts, someone is being paid whether the room is full or not.

£4.5 billion became £15.3 billion. Somebody signed that. The least the country deserves is to know who, and to watch them answer for it.
