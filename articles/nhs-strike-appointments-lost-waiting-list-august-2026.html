171,776 Cancelled Appointments: The Strike Bill Patients Are Quietly Paying

NHS analysis estimates that strikes in 2025/26 cost 171,776 appointments and procedures. The waiting list has come down from its peak, which deserves saying. It still stands at 7.27 million cases, with around 106,000 people waiting more than a year.

Somewhere in England there is a woman who was told in the spring that her hip operation had a date. Then she was told it did not. Then she was told it did again, later. She is one of an estimated 171,776 appointments and procedures that NHS analysis says were lost to industrial action across 2025/26, and she is not a number to herself.

That figure deserves more attention than it has had, because it is the part of the waiting list debate that both sides find inconvenient. Ministers would rather talk about the total coming down. Unions would rather talk about pay. The patient in the middle just wants the operation.

Where the Waiting List Actually Stands

Let me give the honest numbers rather than the convenient ones. As at June 2026, the referral-to-treatment waiting list stood at around 7.27 million cases, representing roughly 6.15 million individual patients — many people are waiting for more than one thing. Of those, about 2.48 million had been waiting more than eighteen weeks, and around 106,000 had been waiting more than a year.

Here is the part I will not pretend away: that list has come down from its peak, and earlier in 2026 NHS England reported one of the largest single-month reductions in years. Progress is progress. Anyone who tells you the NHS has done nothing but deteriorate is not reading the data.

But look at the median. The middle patient on that list waits 11.9 weeks to start treatment. Before the pandemic, in June 2019, the median was 7.5 weeks. Seven years on, the typical wait is still more than half as long again as it was — and the Government's own pledge, to restore the 92 per cent constitutional standard, is not due to be met until March 2029.

So the trajectory is better and the destination is still a decade from where we started. Both things are true.

The Strike Question Nobody Will Answer Straight

Into that picture arrives industrial action, and with it 171,776 lost appointments in a single year. Behind each one is a theatre slot that went unused, a consultant clinic that ran empty, a scan bay idle, and a patient told to wait longer for something they were already waiting too long for.

I have no interest in demonising nurses or junior doctors. Many are paid poorly for demanding, technical, unpleasant work, and the people I meet in the health service in Lancashire are not militants — they are exhausted. NHS staff have repeatedly kept emergency cover running through disputes, and the Confederation's own data shows the effort that took.

But the arithmetic does not care about anyone's good intentions. A health service that loses the better part of two hundred thousand appointments a year to disputes cannot clear a seven million case backlog, no matter what target is written into the mandate. And the reason those disputes keep recurring is that no government of either colour has been willing to have the honest conversation about what the NHS employs people to do, what it pays them, and where the money comes from.

Instead we get another reorganisation. Another structure. Another set of letterheads. The one thing Whitehall reliably produces when it cannot produce doctors.

Reorganisation Is Not Reform

Every few years the answer to the NHS is a new architecture. Bodies merge, bodies split, logos change, and thousands of staff spend a year working out who they now report to instead of treating patients. The waiting list is indifferent to all of it.

What moves a waiting list is capacity actually used: theatres running full days, diagnostics running evenings and weekends, beds not blocked by patients who are medically fit but have nowhere to be discharged to. That last one is a social care failure wearing an NHS uniform, and it has been ducked by every administration since I was old enough to vote.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK would put the money at the front line rather than the org chart. That means a hard freeze on structural reorganisations, so that management time goes into throughput instead of transition plans, and a published audit of how many people in the health service are employed in roles that never see a patient.

It means treating the discharge problem as the bottleneck it is, with social care capacity funded to release the beds that currently sit occupied by people who no longer need a hospital. Every blocked bed is an operation not performed upstream.

On pay and disputes, we would deal in specifics rather than symbolism: settle where the case is made, be candid where it is not, and stop pretending that a service losing 171,776 appointments a year to strikes is a service in stable industrial relations. Pretending the problem away has cost patients more than confronting it ever would.

The list is falling. It is not falling fast enough, and 106,000 people have now been waiting more than a year for treatment in one of the richest countries on earth. That is the measure that matters, and it is the one nobody in government wants to lead with.
