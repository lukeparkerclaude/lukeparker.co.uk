Four Ministers Walk Out: Starmer's Government in Total Freefall

Four ministers resigned in a single day. Ninety Labour MPs want him gone. Yet Keir Starmer clings to office while his cabinet crumbles around him. This is not a government any more — it is a hostage situation.

You don't lose four ministers in a single day and call it normal politics. You don't have ninety of your own MPs publicly demanding your exit and call it a mandate. Yesterday Keir Starmer's premiership entered its terminal phase, even if the man at the top still refuses to read the room. Four junior ministers — Miatta Fahnbulleh, Jess Phillips, Alex Davies-Jones and Zubir Ahmed — walked out of the front door of Downing Street. Four parliamentary private secretaries followed them out the back.

This is not a wobble. This is a collapse.

The Numbers Tell the Story

Around ninety Labour MPs — more than a fifth of the parliamentary party — have now publicly called on the Prime Minister to either resign or set out a timetable for his departure. That is not a fringe. That is a full-blown rebellion on the backbenches of his own party, and it is growing by the hour.

Yes, more than a hundred MPs have signed a counter-letter claiming "this is no time for a leadership contest". But anyone who has ever sat in a council chamber knows what that kind of letter really means. It is a signal of weakness, not strength. When you need to organise your loyalists into a written show of hands to prop up the leader, the leader has already lost the room. Real authority does not need a petition.

Starmer told his cabinet yesterday morning that the country "expects us to get on with governing" and that "that is what I am doing". The trouble is — the country expects rather more than that. It expects a Prime Minister who is actually in charge of his own ministers, his own party, and his own border.

Why Are They Really Resigning?

The official line is "personal reasons" and "time to spend with family". Anyone who has spent five minutes in politics knows what that means. These ministers can read a swingometer. They saw what Reform UK did to Labour councillors last week. They saw 1,400 Labour seats wiped out. They saw old Labour heartlands like Sunderland, Tameside and Wigan turn turquoise. And they made a calculation: better to jump now than to be associated with whatever wreckage Starmer leaves behind.

Jess Phillips's resignation is especially telling. She is not a marginal figure. She is a household name on the Labour left, the kind of MP whose departure cannot be brushed off as a routine reshuffle. When she walks, others will follow. And Zubir Ahmed — a known Wes Streeting ally — quitting a health ministerial role tells you exactly where the leadership challenge is being co-ordinated from.

A Prime Minister Who Has Lost the Country

Let's be honest about what we are watching. The voters delivered their verdict on 7 May. They were given a chance, in dozens of council chambers across England, to tell Labour what they thought of the last two years. They thought it was a disaster. Stealth taxes. Open borders. Energy bills still 35% higher than promised. NHS waiting lists at record highs. A government that picks fights with farmers, pensioners and small business owners while bending over backwards to placate everyone else.

The local elections were a referendum on Starmer's government, and the public returned a "no" the size of a continent. Yet the man himself sits in Number 10 insisting nothing has really happened. That isn't leadership. That is denial.

The Markets Are Watching

What worries me most is not the political theatre, but the practical consequences. Britain has a Prime Minister who no longer commands his cabinet. We have a Chancellor — Rachel Reeves — who has already been openly rumoured to be on the way out, with reports of an "acrimonious split" with Number 10. We have an immigration crisis that has produced over 200,000 small-boat arrivals since 2018. And we have a tax burden heading north of 40% of GDP.

This is exactly the moment that bond markets, foreign investors and ordinary businesses look at Britain and ask: who is actually in charge here? The honest answer is — nobody.

What Reform UK Would Do

Reform UK offers something this Labour government cannot: a clear direction and the will to deliver it. A 20,000 personal allowance to take low-paid workers out of income tax altogether. A genuine border policy, not £662 million handed to the French for nothing. A serious deportation plan. The triple lock honoured, not raided. The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero abolished and energy bills brought back down to earth.

The country isn't crying out for another Labour leadership contest. The country is crying out for a general election. Streeting versus Burnham versus Rayner makes no difference if all three of them voted for the same disastrous Budget, the same stealth taxes, the same open-border policy. Changing the captain doesn't refloat the ship.

Starmer should call an election. He won't, of course. He'll cling on, hoping the rebels fold. But every day he stays, Reform UK's case gets stronger. We told you what would happen. Voters are now seeing it for themselves.
