Justice Has to Be Blind. Too Many People No Longer Believe It Is.

The oldest promise our justice system makes is a simple one: everyone is equal before the law, and justice is blind. Break that promise and you break faith with the public. Right now, too many people have stopped believing it, and they are not being paranoid. They are paying attention.

There is one principle that holds the whole of British justice together, and it is painted on the statue above the Old Bailey. Justice is blind. The law does not care who you are, what you believe, what colour your rosette is or what god you pray to. Treat two people who do the same thing in two different ways, and you have not bent the rules. You have broken the thing itself.

The trouble is that a growing number of people no longer believe the promise is being kept. And before anyone dismisses that as conspiracy, remember this: the government agreed with them, and had to pass a law.

This Is Not a Fringe Worry. Parliament Legislated On It.

Last year the Sentencing Council issued guidance suggesting that courts should normally order a pre-sentence report for offenders from certain groups, including those from an ethnic, cultural or religious minority background. In plain English, your background would help decide how your sentence was approached. It was branded, accurately, "two-tier justice." The Justice Secretary intervened, the guidance was suspended the day before it was due to take effect, and Parliament then passed the Sentencing Guidelines (Pre-Sentence Reports) Act 2025 to forbid personal characteristics such as race, religion and culture from being used in that way.

Think about what that means. The system got so close to formally building different tiers into sentencing that the government had to legislate to stop it. This is not a problem invented by the internet. It is a problem the state itself admitted was real.

Why Confidence Keeps Draining Away

The public does not read sentencing guidelines. What they do is watch how the system behaves, case by case, and ask a simple question: is this being treated as seriously as it would be if it were me, or someone like me, in the dock?

Nothing tests that faith more than how we treat attacks on the police. When a sitting local councillor in Birmingham was charged this summer with five assault offences, including the alleged assault of three police officers, many people were surprised to see the case proceed with the defendant granted unconditional bail. Let me be completely clear, because it matters: she denies the charges, she is entitled to the presumption of innocence, she is entitled to bail, and she is entitled to a fair trial. Nothing here suggests otherwise, and the court will decide the facts, not a website. The point is not her guilt or innocence. The point is that cases like this are exactly what the public watches, and from the outside they ask whether an assault on a police officer is always treated with the seriousness it deserves, whoever stands accused.

Back the People Who Protect Us

An assault on a police officer is not an ordinary assault. It is an attack on the thin line that stands between order and chaos, carried out against people who run towards danger so the rest of us can run away from it. If the public comes to believe that attacking an officer carries different consequences depending on who you are or what cause you claim to serve, then respect for the uniform erodes, and every officer on every shift is left a little less safe. That is the real cost of two-tier justice. It is measured in confidence lost.

Where Reform UK Stands

One law. For everyone. Blind to politics, blind to background, applied the same to all. Reform UK would keep personal characteristics out of sentencing for good, make assaults on police and emergency workers carry consequences that actually match the crime, and work to rebuild the public confidence that equal justice depends on. Justice does not only have to be done. It has to be seen to be done, by everyone, or the whole thing falls apart. The blindfold on that statue is not a weakness. It is the entire point. We take it off at our peril.
